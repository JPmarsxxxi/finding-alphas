Volatility selling (on equity indices)

• Selling volatility using equity index options is the purest
example of selling lottery tickets that pay off (to the buyer) in bad
times. Such strategies deserve high risk premia because large
losses (spiking volatility) tend to coincide with market crashes.
Understanding this strategy is important even if one does not
trade it. Many investors do trade related strategies, such as carry.
• The strategy’s high SR over 20 years between the 1987 and
2008 crashes also reflects a peso problem—the absence of that
rare large-loss event from the sample. A proximate reason for the
strategy’s long-run success is the persistent pattern that implied
volatilities of index options tend to exceed realized volatilities by
2% to 4%.
• For single-stock options, the average gap between implied
and realized volatilities is nearer to zero. This contrast is
suggestive of a correlation risk premium rather than a variance
(volatility) risk premium. Correlation trading—selling volatility
on an equity index and buying volatility on its single-stock
constituents—has been historically profitable but loses money
when realized correlations spike up.
• In addition, covered-call-writing strategies, which are in
the category of volatility selling strategies, have been profitable in
the past but their performance partly reflects long equity market
exposure and not just volatility risk exposure.
• The theoretical sign of skewness preferences can be
debated but the market pricing of options across strike prices
suggests that most investors prefer positive skewness: protective,
out-of-the-money index puts (“crash protectors”) and speculative
OTM calls on single-stock options (“lottery tickets”) are
especially expensive.
• As always, both rational and irrational explanations are
possible for any observed regularities.

15.1 INTRODUCTION

The last dynamic strategy style I describe is volatility selling (see cube).
Volatility 
selling 
is 
a 
form 
of

financial 
insurance 
(tail 
risk
insurance) that warrants some long-run compensation. In particular, selling
volatility on equity indices provided historically attractive payoffs for two
decades from the crash of October 1987 until the fall of 2008—with high
but misleading long-run Sharpe ratios. Selling volatility on other asset
classes has also earned reasonably consistent profits, whereas it is less clear
that selling volatility on individual equities is rewarded:

• Index volatility selling is the archetype of “selling lottery tickets
whose

tend 
to 
materialize 
during 
bad 
times.” 
Negative
skewness (losses are rare but large) and the timing of losses justify a
positive risk premium and explain why the Sharpe ratio overstates the
attractiveness of such strategies.
• Volatility selling is obviously risky but can be a valid business
strategy for investors who can really afford to provide such insurance.
Most investors do not directly trade this archetype but they do trade its
close cousins that come in many guises. Credit and FX carry strategies,
various hedge fund strategies, the insurance business, and even the
equity risk premium (based on rare-disaster motivation or simply on
the limited liability feature in equities) are all related to volatility
selling.
• While relatively few investors trade options, the simplicity and
nonlinear payoffs of options can make them more informative about
investor expectations and preferences than the prices of more bundled
linear assets. We can especially learn about the pricing of higher
moment risks (volatility, skew, kurtosis/fat tails/jumps, correlation) in
option markets and try to extrapolate this knowledge to the pricing of
other asset markets (see also Chapter 19).
• This chapter is a bit more technical than most of the rest of the
book, while cutting corners in the eyes of some experts. Readers can
also skip this chapter without loss of continuity.
Trading options vs. trading volatility

Options are the classic example of derivatives with nonlinear payoffs
(compared with the underlying asset), while futures are the classic example
of derivatives with linear payoffs. Only capturing the upside (buying calls)
or protecting from downside (buying puts) sounds appealing but these
benefits come with a cost: you pay a premium that you can fully lose and
that—in a large proportion of cases (depending on the strike price)—you
actually expect to lose. In long option positions, buyers’ losses materialize
through the time decay of the paid option premium, akin to a negative carry.
If “nothing happens” over the life of the option, it expires worthless. This
cost may be offset by capital gains if “something happens” and the option
ends in the money, and the option buyer can pocket the difference between
the market price and strike price, minus the purchase price of the option:
• If investors buy only calls or puts, directional exposure is
typically more important than volatility exposure. The payoff profile is
asymmetric.
• A straddle position—long both at-the-money (ATM) calls and
puts—has 
no 
directional 
bias 
and 
a 
symmetric 
payoff 
profile. 
A
straddle is a type of long-volatility position, but it is not a pure bet on
high volatility if it is bought and held. It will be profitable if the price
of the underlying asset makes a large shift in either direction, but it can
lose money despite high interim volatility if the underlying price ends
near its starting level. A buy-and-hold straddle is a bet on a large price
move—not on high volatility; in options jargon, gamma risk rather
than vega risk.
• Purer volatility or variance exposure (i.e., exposure to vega risk)
can be achieved via trading delta-hedged straddles or variance swaps.
Both 
approaches 
capture 
the 
difference 
between 
implied
volatility/variance (IV) and realized volatility/variance (RV) over the
life of the contract.

I assume that the reader is familiar with basic option-pricing terminology.
Here is a recap on option pricing using the replication argument: in the
classic 
Black–Scholes–Merton 
(BSM) 
world 
[1] 
option 
traders 
can
continuously delta-hedge the market-directional (delta) risk of their options
by trading offsetting amounts of the underlying security, and thus create a
synthetic risk-free asset. No-arbitrage arguments imply that the option
should be priced so that the expected return of the delta-hedged option
position equals the risk-free rate. This is the reason that derivative pricing is
consistent with risk-neutral probabilities even when real-world investors
may be risk averse. Specifically, risk-neutral pricing of a derivative off its
underlying asset does not require assuming that real-world investors are risk
neutral. Risk aversion does not matter if positions could be hedged. A
standalone position in an equity option should earn a risk premium for its
equity market risk, say based on its CAPM beta (positive for calls, negative
for puts). But for the delta-hedged position, the betas of the option and the
stock just offset each other, leaving a zero premium for a zero-beta position.
This observation, together with assuming a particular asset-pricing model,
allows analysts to switch between (i) real-world pricing where expected
cash flows are discounted with an appropriate discount rate that includes a
risk premium (which may be hard to define and measure) and (ii) risk-
neutral pricing where expected cash flows are discounted with the riskless
rate.

Delta hedging involves offsetting a call option’s delta through dynamic
stock trading in a way that eliminates directional exposure. Delta hedging a
sold (short) option position is a “buy-high, sell-low” strategy that will be
more costly the longer the absolute distance that the stock price moves
during 
the 
life 
of 
the 
option 
(between 
discrete 
hedge 
rebalancings).
Consequently, short option positions benefit from lower realized volatility.
Under 
idealized 
BSM 
conditions, 
a 
delta-hedged 
short 
at-the-money
straddle’s 
return 
reflects 
the 
difference 
between 
contracted 
implied
volatility and realized volatility over the option’s life. In reality, market
price moves are not continuous, hedging is costly and done discretely, and
volatility is not constant—all of which make delta hedging imperfect.
Therefore, delta hedging does not capture pure volatility risk as cleanly as
variance swaps do.

A variance swap is a contract that pays the difference between the
subsequent realized swap rate (based on actual variance) and the contracted
or fixed swap rate (mainly based on the market’s expectation of variance at
the time the swap is entered into). A sold variance swap is profitable if
future realized variance turns out lower than the contracted variance swap
rate. By construction, variance swaps are not subject to various unintended
risks to which delta-hedged straddles are exposed—gamma risk between
discrete rebalancings, the possibility of jumps (model risk), and the impact
of market frictions such as illiquidity and trading costs. Variance swaps
have become popular in recent years but are not yet available for all assets.

Interpreting implied volatilities

It is interesting for a book on expected returns that the famous Black–
Scholes option-pricing formula does not contain expected returns. Under
BSM assumptions, options deserve no risk premium beyond their equity
market beta exposure, and delta-hedged positions deserve no premium, full
stop. When BSM assumptions fail, however, the option may need to offer a
(volatility) 
risk 
premium 
beyond 
that 
earned 
by 
its 
relation 
to 
the
underlying. Delta-risky positions do warrant a risk premium proportional to
their delta (and equity market beta, if the CAPM holds).

The 
cross-sectional 
implication 
of 
BSM 
assumptions 
(constant
volatilities, no market frictions, etc.) is that all options across all strike
prices should have the same implied volatility. Thus, real-world deviations
from BSM assumptions should explain observed smile effects and other
apparent 
violations 
of 
no-arbitrage 
pricing. 
(The 
smile 
refers 
to 
the
appearance of a plot of implied volatility on the y-axis against strike prices
or option moneyness on the x-axis, where moneyness is the degree to which
an option is in the money.)

The BSM analysis taught investors to infer implied parameters such as
implied volatility from market prices, always given a particular model or set
of assumptions. In the risk-neutral world, implied volatility reflects the
market’s volatility expectations. In the real world, this implied parameter
may also reflect some risk premia in addition to volatility expectations.

Thus, analogous to the way a term spread reflects the market’s rate
expectations plus required bond risk premia, implied volatilities also reflect
the market’s volatility expectations plus some volatility-related risk premia.
Any decomposition requires a specified model and empirical estimation,
and any resulting estimates are noisy. As a convenient simplification, it is
common to interpret excess implied volatility over realized volatility as a
volatility risk premium. Empirically, this approximation works well, at least
for assessing historical average premia. If we recognize the time-varying
nature of the volatility premium, careful estimation of the two components
becomes important, preferably using more robust “model-free” approaches.

By market convention, implied volatilities are often quoted based on the
B-S formula and reflect its underlying assumptions. Unequal B-S implied
volatilities across strike prices (smile or smirk) may be interpreted as
market recognition of prevalent non-normalities and other deviations from
B-S assumptions. A symmetric volatility smile could be explained by fat
tails 
in 
the 
real-world 
asset 
return 
distribution; 
while 
an 
asymmetric
skew/smirk could be explained by greater downside volatility (the market’s
greater propensity to jump downwards rather than upwards). Since the 1987
equity market crash, the skew for equity index options has been so extreme
that investor preferences likely have contributed to it. Either a rational risk
premium (such as portfolio insurance preferences leading to greater risk
aversion after down markets) or investors’ irrational crash fears could
explain 
the 
excess 
of 
skew 
implied 
in 
index 
option 
prices 
over 
that
observed in realized market returns.

15.2 HISTORICAL PERFORMANCE OF VOLATILITY-
TRADING STRATEGIES

Covered-call-writing vs. volatility/variance-selling strategies

Perhaps the most popular option strategy is covered call writing (CCW):
holding a stock or a stock index and writing a call against it. Effectively, the
call writer sacrifices some upside exposure in exchange for receiving option
premium income, while maintaining the downside (except, of course, for
the cushion from the premium income). For CCW using ATM calls on the
S&P 500 index (where the overall position has a beta near 0.5), roughly half
of the strategy’s long-run return is related to directional risk (the equity risk
premium) and half to the fact that implied volatility is typically higher than
realized volatility (the volatility risk premium). Between 1994 and 2005,
each component earned 24 bp per month, the latter component reflecting
the 2% average IV–RV gap (see Figelman, 2008). For out-of-the-money
(OTM) options written, the equity beta component is higher (more upside
exposure is kept) and the volatility component is lower. There is some
evidence that the strategy is more profitable if OTM options are used,
perhaps due to a better capture of the equity premium and more expensive
options. The strategy is more profitable if very-short-dated options are used
(wider IV–RV gap, on average) and is likely to be less profitable if covered
call writing is done on individual stocks (narrower IV–RV gap, on average).

CCW strategies performed extremely well during the long bull market
since the 1980s, resulting in various products offered to institutional and
retail investors. Figure 15.1 and Table 15.1 analyze the performance and
risk statistics of various strategies. The BXM and BXY indices, produced
by the Chicago Board of Exchange, simulate CCW strategies using ATM
and 
OTM 
call 
options. 
BXM

BXY

especially 
attractive

effectively giving up the upside on the equity index in exchange for
receiving 
an 
option 
premium. 
Historically, 
these

outperformed the S&P 500 on a volatility-adjusted basis, but their tail risk

CCW strategies are hardly diversifiers away from equity indices—their
correlations are very high, indeed nearly perfect for down moves. CCW
return volatility is mainly driven by equity returns (directional exposure),
especially if done with OTM options. Absolute return seekers who look for
less correlated return sources should prefer variance swaps that largely
avoid directional exposure. Variance swap returns are mainly driven by the
difference between implied and realized volatilities (squared). However,
even

way to buy or sell volatility until variance swap contracts were created and
their OTC markets became liquid.

15.3 TWEAKS/REFINEMENTS

Certain types of volatility exposures appear better rewarded than others. I
introduce correlation and skewness premia and return to these topics later in
this chapter and in Chapter 19.

Correlation 
premium. 
Equity 
index 
option 
selling 
is 
much 
more
profitable than individual equity option selling. The implied volatilities of
equity indices trade consistently at a premium over realized volatilities, but
the same is not true for single-stock options. This difference may be
explained by the correlation risk premium. Traders may hedge index option
sales with purchases of single-stock options in the same index basket. The
resulting correlation or dispersion trades have attractive historical returns
but they suffer when correlation across stocks rises—typically during crises
and in volatile bear markets.

For readers unfamiliar with correlation trading, basic statistics may give
helpful intuition. Recall that market volatility reflects weighted average
volatility across stocks and the weighted average correlation between them.
We can infer higher implied correlation when index option volatility rises
faster than the average volatility of single-stock options constituting the
index.

Given that a dispersion trading strategy can hedge away delta and vega
risks (equity and volatility risks) and has a reasonably symmetric return
distribution, correlation risk seems to be the main explanation for the
richness of equity index options and thus the profitability of option selling.
The correlation premium could be motivated by deterioration of investment
opportunities 
when 
diversification 
becomes 
harder 
or 
by 
timevarying
disagreement 
among 
investors. 
Buraschi–Kosowski–Trojani 
(2010)
document a higher SR for correlation selling than for variance selling
(roughly 3 vs. 1.5) over a 13-year sample period, which includes the
autumn 2008 crisis, as well as smaller losses during crises. SRs may be
overstated as they are based on estimated synthetic variance and correlation
swap prices or mid-market quotes, which may differ from traded market
prices. Also, Driessen–Maenhout–Vilkov (2009) report a higher SR (“only”
0.73) for the correlation strategy than for index volatility selling, as well as
better 
skew 
and 
kurtosis 
characteristics. 
Limits-to-arbitrage 
arguments
(trading costs, margin requirements, etc.) may prevent most investors from
exploiting the correlation premium.

Skewness premium. OTM index put options have a larger gap between
implied 
and 
realized 
volatilities 
than 
ATM 
options, 
they 
appear
systematically expensive compared with various fair value metrics, and
their historical average returns are particularly negative. Option traders
already recognized in the 1970s that implied volatilities across strike prices
exhibit a smile pattern—higher volatilities for OTM puts and calls than for
ATM options. After the 1987 crash, a highly asymmetric smirk or skew
replaced a symmetric smile for index options.

Single-stock option pricing provides further hints of lottery seeking. Ni
(2006) estimates that OTM calls on individual equity options have had large
negative average returns over 1996–2005: around—30% per month based
on mid-market (average of bid and ask) prices. They also underperformed
calls with lower strike prices by a similar amount. High transaction costs
and noisy price data on single-stock options may undermine these results,
however.

Other types of refinements include timing volatility positions based on
the size of ex ante opportunities and/or hedging volatility positions via
selective long–short strategies. Timing overlays on volatility selling could
include simple rules that have been effective in the past—say, do not sell
volatility if current implied volatility is well below the “normal” (past
average) implied, recent realized, or predicted volatility, where predicted
volatility is based on a GARCH-type model or other forecasting model.
Hedging may involve long–short strategies that sell volatility in markets
where it is rich and buy volatility in markets where it is cheap.

It is not clear whether volatility selling is profitable for all single stocks,
because many studies show no consistently positive IV–RV gap, unlike the
situation that prevails with index options. However, Cao–Han (2009) show
that covered call writing on high-volatility stocks earned a 2% higher
monthly return (between 1996 and 2006) than covered call writing on low-
volatility stocks. Moreover, delta-hedged long option positions tend to have
negative 
average 
returns 
that 
are 
monotonically 
lower 
the 
higher 
the
volatility 
of 
the 
underlying 
stock. 
This 
evidence 
is 
consistent 
with 
a
volatility risk premium that is proportional to the level of asset volatility.
Option selling is thus more profitable on more volatile stocks (which tend
to be less liquid and more costly to trade), on stocks with a large IV–RV
gap 
(expensive 
options), 
on 
stocks 
with 
large 
end-user 
demand, 
and
especially 
profitable 
on 
past 
loser 
stocks, 
while 
exposure 
to 
market
volatility risk is not important.

15.4 THE REASONS VOLATILITY SELLING IS
PROFITABLE

Should we expect to make money by option selling or option buying?

Taking a step back, it is not clear a priori whether systematic option buying
or option selling is a better strategy. The answer depends on the empirical
pricing of options—“Are they actuarially fair prices?” or “Do they provide
an edge to buyers or sellers?”—and ultimately on investor preferences. The
intuitive idea that volatility sellers provide insurance and should receive
some compensation seems correct. Returns on volatility-selling strategies
exhibit negative skew and high kurtosis (fat tails), as frequent small gains
are interspersed with rare large losses. Extensions of mean variance analysis
to higher moments typically suggest that natural investor preferences are to
like high average returns and positive skewness (first and third moments of
return distribution) and to dislike high volatilities and kurtosis (second and
fourth moments). Skewness preference and kurtosis aversion may thus
make long options “expensive” and volatility selling generally profitable.
Even 
without 
such 
preferences, 
finance 
theory 
suggests 
that 
volatility
selling should carry a positive risk premium if its losses tend to coincide
with equity market losses and other bad times.

A tendency for volatility selling to be profitable does not make it always
profitable. Competitive forces will limit any systematic richness of options.
Speculators will be attracted by the excess profitability of protection selling
and yield-seeking investors will do more covered call writing. Moreover,
Taleb (2004) argues for an opposite skewness preference bias that may have
become more relevant as delegated fund managers control a larger share of
global wealth. The case of liking negative skewness is more plausible for
delegated managers whose performance-based compensation may induce
them to seek volatility. Less scrupulous managers may also prefer a high
probability of positive returns so that they can accumulate large fees before
a 
rare 
disaster 
materializes, 
especially 
since 
overstated 
smoothness 
of
returns can boost the Sharpe ratio and attract larger AUM until the blowup
occurs.

Main challenges and main explanations

The three best historical trading opportunities and thus the three main
empirical challenges for theories to explain are (i) profits from index
volatility selling or dispersion trading, (ii) richness of OTM index puts, and
(iii) 
richness 
of 
OTM 
calls 
on 
single 
stocks. 
Here 
are 
some 
rational
explanations and alternatives for each challenge:

(i) The persistent significant average difference between implied and
realized 
volatilities 
(or 
variances), 
often 
defined 
as 
a 
volatility 
risk
premium, may reflect a combination of
• compensation for systematic risk (volatility risk, and maybe also
jump, skew, or correlation risk);
• price pressures from investor supply and/or demand;
• 
biased 
forecasts 
of 
realized 
volatility 
(a 
peso

an
unrepresentative 
sample

or 
learning 
amidst 
structural

Carr–Wu (2009) make the case for a systematic variance risk premium.
They regress single-stock return variances on S&P 500 index variance to
estimate variance betas and find that stocks with higher variance betas are
associated with higher variance risk premia (a wider gap between implied
and realized variances). Apparently, investors are willing to accept some
losses in exchange for hedging away market volatility spikes. The variance
risk premium may reflect (1) the empirical linkage between stock return and
variance and/or (2) stochastic volatility as a distinct risk factor. Stock
market variance tends to be higher during down markets; thus long (short)
variance positions have a negative (positive) stock market beta. Because
this beta risk can explain empirically only a small portion of observed
profits 
from 
variance 
selling 
(variance 
risk 
premia), 
an 
independent
variance risk factor seems to be the key contributor. This evidence is
relatively weak; stronger evidence for a volatility premium comes from the
finding 
that 
volatility 
selling 
is 
more 
profitable 
for 
stocks 
with 
high
volatility.

However, 
the 
contrasting 
profitability 
of 
volatility 
selling 
in 
index
options (very profitable) and in single-stock options (unprofitable) argues
against a simple variance risk premium. Stated differently, the presence of a
significantly positive gap between implied and realized volatilities for index
options, and the absence thereof for single stocks, suggests that it is
correlation risk rather than volatility risk that is priced. Other evidence in
Chapter 19 about the ambiguous relation between expected returns and
volatilities points to the same explanation.

From a theoretical perspective, the tendency for a strategy of selling
volatility and/or correlation risk to lose money in bad times suggests that
these risk premia should be negative (so that selling these risks is profitable
in the long run).

(ii) In modeling terminology, the smirk in implied volatilities (higher
Black–Scholes

volatilities 
for 
OTM

and 
lower 
implied

pricing asymmetries. Main alternative explanations are analogous to those
above:
• A risk premium for volatility, correlation, and/or skew exposure
may 
reflect 
investor 
demand 
for 
downside 
protection.

• Supply–demand imbalance due to an exaggerated need for

may

have

• A peso problem may be a partial explanation. No crashes of the

has assigned a small probability to such an event. Some observers
view this as an irrationally high crash expectation but one can also
argue 
that 
realized 
outcomes 
between 
1988

2007

Was

Assessing irrational and rational explanations

Irrational explanations rely on (1) the demand from irrational investors
(end-users) distorting option prices and (2) the inability of rational risk-
neutral arbitrageurs or market-makers to offset these distortions. In contrast
to the ideal BSM world, in the real world market-makers cannot perfectly
hedge 
their 
option 
positions. 
Noncontinuous 
pricing, 
jumps, 
stochastic
volatility, and trading costs as well as capital constraints make market-
makers sensitive to unhedgeable option risks and cause them to require
compensation for bearing them. Since market-maker actions no longer
offset end-user demand biases, it is important to assess what those biases
are. New academic studies document many demand regularities that are
consistent with observed pricing regularities:
• End-users tend to be net long index options and net short single-
stock options. This pattern partly explains the richness of equity index
options.
• End-users are most active in single-stock OTM calls and index
OTM puts. Perhaps not surprisingly, both option types are shown to
produce especially low future returns. Single-stock calls may be used
both in covered call writing and in speculative trading. Index puts are
primarily used as protection vehicles.
• End-user demand for index options exhibits a more pronounced
skew than their demand for single-stock options. This is consistent
with the exceptional richness of OTM index puts.
• 
More 
generally, 
there 
is 
a 
positive 
cross-sectional 
relation
between option demand and option richness.
• Only a small portion of option market activity is related to
volatility 
trading. 
Rather, 
options 
are 
used 
as 
a 
means 
to 
take
directional positions—say, by facilitating short selling or leverage—or
as a source of additional income.
• 
Poor 
institutional 
investor 
sentiment 
coincides 
with 
more
negative skew for index options, consistent with greater protection
demand. 
In 
contrast, 
good 
sentiment 
among 
individual 
investors
coincides with higher demand for single-stock call options, especially
lottery-like OTM options, making them more expensive.

Analysis 
of 
end-user 
demand 
does 
not 
reveal 
their 
motivations 
or
rationality. Most studies conclude that irrational mispricing contributes
importantly 
to observed option 
market regularities. 
The rational camp
responds that risk stories can explain a surprisingly large part of observed
returns without resorting to irrationality—and that various market frictions
can make exploiting any remaining opportunities difficult.

Specifically, Broadie–Chernov–Johannes (2009) argue that options are
often thought to be mispriced because the performance metrics that are used
(Sharpe 
ratios 
and 
CAPM 
alphas) 
are 
ill-suited 
for 
option 
analysis,
especially over short samples. After documenting the huge challenge for
rational models—massively negative average returns for long index puts,
losses of 30% per month, or worse, as noted earlier—they proceed to show
that 
standard 
option-pricing 
models 
can 
largely 
explain 
these 
average
returns. OTM puts are especially highly levered positions on the underlying
index; during a period of high realized equity premium, OTM puts with
large 
negative 
betas 
can 
be 
expected 
to 
have 
large 
negative 
returns.
(Annualized returns are on the order of hundreds of percent, but so are
annual volatilities.) Option returns are also sensitive to volatility: lower
volatility 
reduces 
expected 
put 
returns 
(makes 
them 
more 
negative).
Because this relation is nonlinear (concave), time-varying volatility further
reduces expected put returns. These patterns, combined with the difficulty
of estimating expected option returns (sampling uncertainty is exaggerated
for nonlinear payoff distributions), imply that extremely negative average
returns for OTM puts are not statistically different from those generated by
the simple B-S model. Low average returns on ATM straddles (consisting
of long position in both an ATM call and ATM put)—roughly —15% per
month—are more challenging to explain because these positions have zero
equity market betas, so their returns cannot be explained by their levered
directional exposure. Authors need to go beyond the B-S model (or a
stochastic volatility model without risk premia) to explain ATM straddle
returns. Incorporating a jump risk premium and estimation risk can generate
double-digit 
negative 
expected 
returns 
and 
thus 
explain 
ATM 
option
evidence without mispricing. Beyond these explanations, there are wide
bid–ask spreads and other market frictions to consider.

15.5 OTHER ASSETS

Volatility selling appears to be profitable outside equity markets, but not as
profitable (or risky) as with equity index options. Trolle–Schwartz (2008)
study variance risk premia for energy commodities. Between 1996 and
2006 average implied volatility exceeded average realized volatility by 4%
for crude oil and by 3% for natural gas. The SRs from shorting estimated
variance swaps were 0.59 for oil and 0.35 for natural gas (compared with
1.02 for the equity index). Duarte–Longstaff–Yu (2007) document SRs
ranging between 0.0 and 0.8 for strategies that short interest rate volatility.
