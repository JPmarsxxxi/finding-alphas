Inflation factor and inflation premium

• Inflation developments are the main drivers of nominal
(fixed income) asset performance but they also influence stock
markets and other supposedly real assets.
• Long-dated nominal Treasuries have the most consistent
negative inflation sensitivity. The inflation risk premium (IRP) is
the key driver of nominal bond risk premium (BRP). This IRP
may reflect both standalone inflation uncertainty (which appears
to vary with inflation level) and inflation’s covariance with bad
times. Both aspects made the IRP especially high in the early
1980s (when the BRP reached between 3% and 4%) and
especially low in the 2000s (when the BRP hovered near zero).
• Stocks have been negatively related to inflation in the short
term but positively in the long term. The negative relation has
both rational and irrational explanations. This relation flips sign
in pre-World War II data, however, perhaps due to the changing
nature of inflation or the detrimental impact of deflation not seen
since the 1930s. Stock market valuation ratios are highest during
low but positive inflation.
• Inflation-linked bonds and energy futures, as well as (to a
lesser extent) some other commodities and real estate, are good
hedges against inflation—which makes them attractive
diversifiers.
• Deflation is an even more challenging risk for investors
because it hurts all assets except nominal bonds.
• Well-anchored inflation expectations have kept inflation-
related premia negligible for over a decade now, but the situation
may change in the 2010s.

17.1 INTRODUCTION

The 
other 
central 
macro-based 
factor, 
besides 
growth, 
is 
inflation 
or
changes in the general price level (see cube). For the large asset class of
nominal bonds, inflation is the main risk: rising inflation hurts them, falling
inflation helps 
them, 
and 
deflation makes 
them 
excel. High 
or 
rising
inflation also hurts equity markets, at least in the short term. However, this
relation is more complex—equities also suffer amidst deflation—and the
reasons for why a conceptually real asset, such as equities, is empirically
inflation sensitive are debated. A few asset classes such as inflation-linked
bonds, commodities (especially energy-related products), and real estate
benefit from higher inflation, or at least are insensitive to it. What explains
asset sensitivity to inflation and why should such inflation exposure carry a
risk premium?

Why an inflation relation for nominal assets? The answer is obvious for
realized returns—rising inflation expectations raise bond yields and reduce
their prices. The answer is less mechanical for expected real returns of
nominal bonds. Empirically, it is clear that bond yields contain a level-
dependent inflation risk premium (IRP); rising inflation expectations also
boost required IRP and hurt current bond prices, beyond the mechanical
rate expectation impact. The IRP may reflect standalone inflation risk (a
reward for sheer uncertainty about inflation that varies with inflation level)
or more “theoretically kosher” risk explanations: inflation’s covariance with
marginal utility (bad times).

Research into this question used to be split into two directions. The
traditional/ empirical wing analyzed standalone inflation risk while the
theoretical wing studied covariances (the correlation between growth and
inflation rates or between real asset and nominal asset returns). Some new
research combines the two drivers behind inflation premia. The worst of
both worlds occurred in the early 1980s (both premia were record high
when inflation peaked) and the best of both worlds occurred in the mid-
2000s, with low premia on both counts (inflation uncertainty was low,
thanks 
to 
well-anchored 
expectations, 
and 
stock–bond 
correlation 
had
turned negative).

Measuring inflation’s correlation with bad times is challenging. The
correlation between, say, consumption growth and inflation rates is not
terribly interesting due to measurement errors in economic data and to the
fact that the important correlation is more about expected broad movements
than about short-term noise. Let us first glance at decade average inflation
and real growth rates over 200 years in Figure 17.1. It is not obvious
whether the relation is positive or negative. Indeed, the correlation between
these two series is—0.3, while the correlation between annual inflation and
growth rates is +0.3. Table 16.1 showed that the correlation between
monthly changes in consensus forecasts of next year real growth and
inflation is +0.4 (since 1990).

The inconclusive results are underlined when I plot 12-year rolling
correlations 
between 
growth 
and 
inflation 
(year-on-year 
rates). 
These
correlations were positive from the 1870s to the 1950s and then turned
negative until very recently. Broad comovements may be better captured by
asset market data, such as the correlation between quarterly stock and bond
returns (see Figure 17.2). The relation between long-lived real and nominal
assets was mildly positive most of the 20th century, especially in the 1970s
when both bond and stock valuations suffered from high inflation, and in
the 1980s when both benefited from the decline in inflation. Stock–bond
correlation has been negative since the late 1990s and tends to be lower
amidst low inflation, deflation scares, and flight-to-quality episodes (also
see Figure 9.10).

(The construction of these business cycle graphs is described in Chapter
26.) In the past, inflation used to be procyclical, rising during expansions
and falling during contractions, perhaps because it was primarily demand
driven and because money supply was relatively fixed due to the use of a
gold 
standard. 
In 
recent 
decades, 
high 
inflation 
has 
coincided 
with
contraction, perhaps because energy shocks caused both rising inflation and
recession (thus stagflation), and because the Fed responded to threats of
inflation with tight money policies designed to cause a recession. Supply-
driven (scarcity-related) inflation looks especially noxious, prompting high
inflation to coincide with bad times, unlike demand-driven inflation.

Why any inflation relation for real assets such as equities? One can study
the relations between expected, unexpected, or realized stock returns and
expected, 
unexpected, 
or 
realized 
inflation, 
over 
either 
short 
or 
long
horizons. Over a short horizon, the empirical relation between stock returns
and inflation is clearly negative. In Section 8.4.2, I reviewed three main
explanations: 
inflation’s 
ability 
to 
predict 
slow 
economic 
growth, 
an
inflation-related risk premium, and irrational money illusion. Over a long
horizon the empirical relation may be positive; at least ex ante return,
proxied by earnings yield, tends to be elevated during periods of high
expected inflation (but also amidst deflation).

Why is inflation negatively related to real growth? Theory highlights the
real 
economic 
costs 
of 
inflation. 
Coping 
with 
even 
fully 
anticipated
inflation requires some effort—socalled “shoe leather” costs from more
frequent visits to banks, for example. Larger costs come from unexpected
inflation: 
wealth 
redistribution, 
the 
impact 
of 
inflation 
uncertainty 
on
consumption and investment decisions, and the tendency to confuse overall
inflation 
with 
relative 
price 
changes. 
Overall, 
inflation 
and 
inflation
uncertainty lead to misallocation of resources and thus hamper growth.
Empirically, a higher inflation level is associated with greater inflation
variability and uncertainty as well as with slower productivity growth. Time
series analyses on major economies and cross-country comparisons agree:
Barro (1995) studies the experience in over 100 countries over a 30-year
period and documents a positive link between inflation level and inflation
variability as well as a negative link between both of these and economic
growth.

The above linkages are slow moving. More cyclically, monetary policy
has been an important driver of recessions between 1950 and 2001. The Fed
tightens monetary policy at a mature stage of expansion when inflation
pressures intensify, and eases policy after a recession has cooled those
inflation pressures down. Thus, higher inflation predicts slower growth due
to central bank responses, a pattern investors recognize and act upon.

In general, postwar experience has not been at all consistent with the
extreme monetarist view that the real and nominal parts of the economy are
unconnected (i.e., that inflation has no real effects). Inflation level appears
to have been a key driver for many financial and macroeconomic series
postwar—including ex ante real yield, real GDP growth, real profit growth,
productivity, the profit–GDP ratio, the equity market’s P/E ratio—or at least
has shared major trends with them. The peak in inflation (1973–1982)
coincided with adverse real outcomes and asset valuations. However, many
relations have opposite signs in prewar data.

17.2 INFLATION PROCESS—HISTORY, DETERMINANTS,
EXPECTATIONS

It 
is 
important 
to 
recall 
that 
sustained 
inflation 
is 
a 
20th-century
phenomenon. Over centuries there were long periods of inflation but these
were matched by long periods of deflation (see Figure 17.4 extracted from
Reinhart–Rogoff, 2008). For example, the net annual inflation rate in the
U.K. between 1600 and 1900 was near zero, as was the U.S. annual
inflation rate in the 19th century. Other studies suggest that the only period
of sustained inflation in Europe since the 13th century was during the gold
discovery period of the 16th century. The main drivers of pre-industrial
European 
inflation 
were 
demographics 
(population 
expansions 
boosted
demand 
and 
caused 
food 
and 
energy 
prices 
to 
rise) 
and 
wars 
(debt
monetization was an alternative to debt default).

During the gold standard (Britain adopted it in 1717, other developed
countries in the 19th century), inflations and deflations took turns with no
persistence, and long-run inflation expectations were likely near zero most
of the time. Figure 17.1 showed that bouts of inflation coincided with major
wars. Annual inflation rates reached double digits around the American
Civil War and both world wars (but I only show smoother decade averages).
Figure 17.5 compares U.S. government bond yields with estimates of
expected long-term inflation [1]. High real yields (as shown by a wide gap
between yields and inflation expectations in the graph) in the 19th century
partly reflected riskier public debt in the emerging United States; sovereign
defaults were not infrequent.

For those interested in even more distant histories, long-dated bond
yields ranged between 5% and 7% from 1800 to the 1870s, while inflation
—and likely inflation expectations—averaged zero. Default risk rather than
inflation risk was bond investors’ primary concern. The huge debts of
Britain, a legacy of the costly Napoleonic wars, also kept global real yields
high; the superpower of the time worked its way out of these massive debts
slowly over a century. Especially during the gold standard, real yields likely
did not contain much of an inflation uncertainty premium—as any inflation
was seen as temporary and reversible. This mixture also made yield curves
flatter in the 19th century than in the 20th century.

After World War I and suspension of the gold standard, inflation became
more volatile everywhere, but prices could still go down as well as up.
However, in the decades after World War II, the paper (fiat) money regime
and 
a 
growing 
public 
sector 
made 
realized 
inflation 
virtually 
always
positive. 
This 
experience 
caused 
investors 
to 
gradually 
expect 
more
persistently positive inflation rates. In the U.S., wars (Korea and Vietnam)
again coincided with rising fiscal deficits and rising inflation; aggressive
demand man-agement and insufficient understanding of expectations also
contributed. 
After 
the 
Bretton 
Woods 
regime 
(a 
quasi 
gold 
standard)
collapsed in 1971, the major economies were clearly in a fiat money regime
and inflation expectations became further unanchored. The two 1970s’ oil
shocks coincided with productivity decline, while the Fed’s overestimation
of trend output and therefore slack in the economy resulted in overly loose
monetary policy, contributing to the Great Inflation. Under Paul Volcker’s
leadership, which began in 1979, the Fed finally ended and reversed the
decades-long uptrend in inflation—at the cost of deep recessions in 1980–
1982. The decades of the Great Moderation followed, although inflation
scares (1984, 1987, 1990, 1994) did still occur until the Fed’s anti-inflation
credibility was firmly established. Since the late 1990s, long-run inflation
expectations have been remarkably well anchored near 2.5%.

The broad developments above have been global. The most important
difference is that the U.S. never experienced real hyperinflation. Niall
Ferguson (2007) adapts Milton Friedman’s famous quote (that inflation is
always 
and 
everywhere 
a 
monetary 
phenomenon) 
by 
arguing 
that
“hyperinflation is always a political phenomenon,” preceded by political
instability and unrest. Other developed countries suffered hyperinflations
after the world wars (Germany in the early 1920s, Japan in the late 1940s)
[2]. Given this background, it is not surprising that these countries had more
anti-inflationary central banks than the Fed during the Great Inflation. Since
1950, hyperinflations occurred exclusively in developing countries—and,
even 
there, 
they 
were 
increasingly 
rare. 
Disinflation 
became 
a 
global
phenomenon in the 1990s and 2000s as the range of inflation outcomes
across countries narrowed and inflation levels converged toward low single-
digit levels. Enhanced central bank credibility, agreement that inflation
hurts 
growth, 
as 
well 
as 
tailwinds 
from 
globalization, 
deregulation,
competitive 
pressures, 
and 
new 
entrants 
to 
the 
global 
labor 
force
contributed to the low and relatively stable inflation rates in the past decade.

The global recession of 2008–2009 brought deflation back for the first
time in well over a generation. The slack in output and de-leveraging
pressures make deflation rather than inflation the near-term concern for
policymakers. However, dramatically higher public debt and the waning of
disinflationary forces make many observers suspect that the next secular
move in inflation is higher.

Understanding inflation and its drivers

Monetarists link inflation to money supply growth, assuming stable money
demand over time; all other influences are second order or temporary.
Economic 
historians 
emphasize 
the 
role 
of 
wars 
and 
demographics.
Keynesian economists model inflation using the expectations-augmented
Phillips curve: inflation is a function of inflation expectations, and the
output gap (or employment gap vs. the natural rate). A standard Phillips
curve relation suggests that an economy growing near or beyond its full
capacity 
creates 
inflationary 
pressures 
while 
growing 
slack 
predicts
disinflation 
or 
deflation. 
Besides 
demand-driven 
inflation, 
there 
is 
a
possibility of supply–push inflation through energy and currency effects.

Inflation 
expectations 
are 
critical 
because 
well-anchored 
inflation
expectations can stabilize the whole inflation process. There will always be
relative price changes or temporary inflation impulses, but only second-
round 
effects—requests 
for 
price 
or 
wage 
hikes 
that 
compensate 
for
perceived inflation—transform these into persistent inflation. Central bank
credibility is essential to contain second-round effects and to keep any
shocks 
transitory. 
Well-anchored 
inflation 
expectations 
had 
a 
benign
influence during the Great Moderation: empirical studies show that inflation
has become less sensitive to the output gap, and to exchange rate and
energy price developments. The gravitational pull of inflation expectations
means that any deviation of the current inflation rate from the long-run
anchor is seen as temporary, causing inflation shocks to be more transitory
and 
inflation 
expectations 
to 
be 
mean-reverting. 
Besides 
central 
bank
credibility, a lucky environment has also contributed to the virtuous cycle of
disinflation and expectations anchoring. One topical challenge to central
bank credibility is the market’s understanding that high and rising levels of
public indebtedness raise incentives to monetize debt.

Modern central banks recognize the essential role of expectations—as
well 
as 
their 
own 
limited 
understanding 
of 
what 
determines 
inflation
expectations. Thus, central bankers always worry about an unhinging of
inflation expectations that could reverse virtuous developments in recent
decades. Some of this concern is self-serving but justifiable. Well-anchored
inflation 
expectations 
depend 
crucially 
on 
central 
bank 
actions 
and
credibility. In the past two decades, a growing number of central banks have
become independent and/or have begun to explicitly or implicitly target a
particular inflation rate. Inflation management is not the only goal for
central banks, however [3].

Evolving inflation process

For our understanding of inflation expectations and the IRP in the past 50
years it is important to note that the mountain shape of inflation levels since
the mid-20th century was matched by similar uptrends and downtrends in
inflation 
uncertainty 
and 
inflation 
persistence. 
Time-varying 
inflation
uncertainty contributed to evolution of the inflation premium during the
period; 
both 
series 
broadly 
tracked 
the 
inflation 
level. 
Time-varying
inflation persistence had a structural impact on inflation expectations, as
explained below, making those expectations first more sensitive and later
less sensitive to the recent inflation level. Given structural instability, it
seems harsh to judge investors’ poor inflation forecasts as irrational.

Chapter 9 introduced the simple but powerful idea that the IRP in
nominal bonds reflects inflation uncertainty, which varies empirically with
inflation level. Inflation uncertainty has many dimensions but they all seem
broadly level dependent, even outside hyperinflations (although the sign
could flip in deflation). It is not obvious which measures of inflation
uncertainty are most relevant, but all alternatives tell broadly the same story
(see Figure 17.6). A rolling period measure of inflation volatility may be
misleading 
as 
an 
indicator 
of 
inflation 
uncertainty 
because 
short-term
inflation fluctuations captured by such a measure do not matter much; long-
term uncertainty is crucial for bond yields and the IRP. Bond yield volatility
may be a better measure but it also includes real rate volatility. Dispersion
among forecasters in inflation surveys is a third candidate; it too has varied
with 
inflation 
level, 
peaking 
in 
the 
1970s. 
However, 
even 
such
disagreement is not exactly the relevant measure of inflation uncertainty.
Only a few surveys ask questions that reveal participants’ uncertainty about
their own forecasts—a measure of probability distribution—but such data
are 
also 
consistent 
with 
level 
dependence. 
Adding 
the 
evidence 
that
estimated ex ante bond risk premia track expected inflation, it seems fair to
assume that inflation uncertainty and the IRP are both level dependent.

Inflation persistence rose with inflation level until 1980. During the gold
standard, prices could go persistently up or down but the best long-term
forecasts used to be for no change. That is, the price level followed a
random walk and recent inflation had no ability to predict future inflation.
Between the 1950s and the 1970s, as inflation’s persistence gradually rose,
the best time series forecast of future inflation shifted from zero to the most
recent inflation rate. That is, instead of the price level following a random
walk, the inflation rate did so (the statistical persistence parameter rose
from 0 to 1, before reversing after 1980). At least the process did not
become 
more 
explosive 
in 
developed 
markets, 
as 
it 
does 
when
hyperinflation psychology takes hold. Inflation persistence did fall again
through the 1980s, 1990s, and 2000s to levels not seen since the 1960s.

Importantly, we know these structural changes in the inflation process
only in hindsight. Real-time investors had to learn about them gradually.
Statistical analysis suggests that investors systematically underpredicted
inflation in the 1960s and 1970s and then overpredicted it in the 1980s and
1990s. This evidence likely reflects, at least in part, gradual learning about
an evolving process rather than irrational expectations or abnormal risk
premia. Investors in the 1960s did not expect inflation rates to be as
persistent as they turned out to be, based on their experience over preceding
decades. 
Likewise, 
investors 
in 
the 
1980s 
did 
not 
instantly 
see 
that
inflation’s persistence was fading, so they did not expect the swift decay in
inflation level that actually took place.

17.3 INFLATION SENSITIVITY OF MAJOR ASSET
CLASSES AND THE INFLATION PREMIUM

Few 
assets 
benefit 
from 
higher 
inflation. 
Spread 
trades 
between 
real
(inflation-linked) and nominal bonds are the most reliable bets on higher
inflation, but oil futures come close. Inflation-linked bonds, real estate, and
some other commodities are good hedges against inflation—they have a
near-zero short-term inflation beta and they tend to preserve long-term
purchasing power. Stocks tend to have a negative inflation beta in the short
term but a positive one in the long term. Nominal government bonds have a
consistently negative inflation beta. Thus, bond markets are the best place
to study the inflation premium. Caveat: There is some evidence that asset
inflation sensitivities (even signs) may differ between unexpected and
expected inflation, over short and long horizons, and across stable and
volatile 
conditions. 
Martin 
(2010) 
surveys 
the 
literature 
and 
presents
original analysis of various traditional and alternative assets’ inflation-
hedging abilities.

Figure 17.7 shows simple evidence on inflation sensitivity for major
assets with data histories dating back to 1990, thus excluding TIPS (i.e.,
U.S. inflation-indexed Treasury bonds). Assets and strategies are sorted
here by their correlation with the inflation factor. As expected, commodity
futures have benefited most from rising inflation expectations while long-
duration Treasury positions have suffered. The inflation factor here is
monthly change in the consensus forecast of next year U.S. inflation rate
(source Consensus Economics).

Nominal bonds. Higher expected inflation boosts ex ante government
bond 
yields. 
Higher 
unexpected 
inflation 
tends 
to 
boost 
inflation
expectations and also pushes yields higher. Persistent upside surprises in
inflation eat up the purchasing power of fixed cash flows and reduce bonds’
market values (as investors learned the hard way in the 1970s). The flip side
is that disinflation boosts bond prices, and if actual deflation materializes,
bonds may be the only winning asset class. Spread trades between real and
nominal bonds are even more direct bets on changes in inflation rate.

Short-term rates reflect expected inflation and expected real short rates.
Long-term 
bond 
yields 
reflect 
expected 
future 
short 
rates 
(which 
are
principally driven by inflation expectations) and required bond risk premia
(which are principally driven by inflation risk premia). I stress in Chapter 9
that 
an 
inflation-level-dependent 
IRP 
can 
explain 
most 
slow-moving
variation in BRP—the long uptrend before 1980 to a peak above 3% and
the long downtrend thereafter. Ex ante real 10-year yields in Figure 17.8

As noted earlier, the IRP may reflect standalone inflation risk (potential
capital losses on long-duration bonds) or covariance of inflation with
marginal utility (bad times). The model of Campbell–Sunderam–Viceira
(2010) contains both types of factors. Figure 17.6 shows how various
proxies of inflation uncertainty peaked around 1980. This was also the
period when bonds were at their worst from a correlation perspective: high
inflation coincided with a recession (stagflation) rather than an economic
boom, so bond losses occurred in very bad times.

The broad story is that the IRP has the potential to be the main driver of
the BRP of nominal bonds—as indeed happened in the early 1980s when
inflation uncertainty peaked and high inflation coincided with recessions
(stagflation). At such times inflation even makes equity valuations cheap.
Conversely, the disinflation of the 1980s and 1990s contributed to the
expansion of valuation multiples for most asset classes. For nominal bonds,
low and stable inflation—such as has been the case for over a decade now,
when long-term inflation expectations have centered tightly around 2.5%—
is associated with a minuscule IRP and BRP.

Linkers. Inflation-linked bonds such as U.S. TIPS are designed to hedge
inflation, but, holding inflation rates constant, their performance depends on
changes in real yields. Combining a long position in real bonds with a short
position in nominal ones is primarily a bet on rising inflation over the life of
the bonds (or a short-term bet on widening inflation expectations). Yield
spread between nominal and real yields is called break-even inflation (BEI).
BEI is often taken as a measure of the market’s inflation expectations, but
BEI also includes the IRP and any other differences in risk or illiquidity
premia between nominal and real bonds. Before U.S. TIPS were introduced
in 1997, it was commonly thought that the Treasury would save money by
not having to pay the IRP in nominal bonds. This idea was consistent with
theory and with U.K. market experience (where “linkers”, U.K. inflation-
indexed government bonds, had been introduced in the early 1980s): the
IRP should make BEI exceed survey-based inflation expectations.

However, in the early years of TIPS, estimated IRP (the difference
between BEI and consensus inflation forecasts) turned out to be negative.
That is, the Treasury issued TIPS at such high yields (low prices) that it
would have saved costs had it stuck with only issuing nominal Treasuries.
Real yields were quite high (reaching, at one point, 4.3%), surprisingly
close to nominal yields at the time (in the range of 5% to 6%), leaving BEI
(5.5%—4.3 
% 
= 
1.2%) 
much 
lower 
than 
survey-based 
inflation
expectations (2.7%). This measured IRP was negative (1.2%—2.7% =—
1.5%) due to what was eventually understood to be an illiquidity premium
or a novelty premium. With hindsight, it is not too surprising that the pure
inflation premium was low given the stable and low expectations for
inflation, so this premium could be more easily offset and overwhelmed by
an illiquidity premium in a novel asset class that initially lacked natural
buyers.

After a few years, the liquidity of the TIPS market improved and
investors became familiar with the beneficial aspects of TIPS (which really
are the riskless asset for long-term investors who care about real returns), so
the liquidity premium diminished. For a few years, a tiny positive IRP was
apparent (presumably a true IRP near 30–50 bp overwhelmed the illiquidity
premium, which was negative but of even smaller absolute magnitude).
However, during the de-leveraging episode of fall 2008, TIPS cheapened
again dramatically; their yields rose while nominal Treasury yields fell, and
BEI collapsed to zero. Measured IRP became negative again but this
development was more reflective of a spiking illiquidity premium than of
actual deflation fears. This liquidity premium quickly disappeared and the
TIPS market rallied hugely (see Figure 17.9).

Overall, the technical nature of the TIPS market—in particular, the time-
varying illiquidity premium—makes it difficult to use TIPS-derived BEI
and IRP as clear measures of the market’s inflation expectations and
required inflation premium, respectively. BEI has been much more volatile
than consensus inflation forecasts, and its fluctuations are probably better
explained by changing liquidity conditions than by evolution of the pure
inflation premium. Indeed, economists’ consensus forecasts of next decade
inflation have been extremely stable, well within the 2% to 3% range over
the whole time that TIPS have existed, while 10-year BEI has averaged
1.75% while fluctuating within a much broader range (0 to 3%). Still, these
technical distortions have dissipated over time, except during the fall of
2008, and BEI positions—buying (shorting) TIPS and shorting (buying)
duration-matched 
nominal 
Treasuries—remain 
the 
cleanest 
way 
for
investors to take views on inflation prospects.

Equities. Equities are poor inflation hedges over short horizons: rising
inflation tends to push equity prices lower. Various explanations for the
negative inflation beta of equities include a negative inflation growth
relation, an inflation-related risk premium, and an irrational money illusion
caused by inflation (see Chapter 8). The inflation illusion hypothesis can
explain the postwar negative relation between stock returns and inflation
but not the positive relation before World War II. Lee’s (2009) two-regime
hypothesis can explain both periods by noting that the relation between
stock 
returns 
and 
inflation 
depends 
on 
the 
source 
of 
inflation. 
Lee
distinguishes between demand-driven inflation (negative aggregate shocks
to demand in the 1930s and 2008) and supply or scarcity-driven inflation
(the 1970s and 1980s). The former causes a positive relation with stock
returns, the latter a negative relation.

Boudoukh–Richardson (1993) show that, while the short-run relation
between 
nominal 
stock 
returns 
and 
inflation 
is 
negative, 
the 
long-run
relation is positive (using 5-year returns over a 200-year data history in both
the U.S. and Britain). The positive relation is even stronger with ex ante
variables. Figure 8.9 revealed a surprisingly strong relation between equity
market earnings yield and inflation level. The simple story would be that
high inflation makes equity markets cheap. However, if we use smoothed
P/E ratios, the relation is quite nonlinear. Deflation can be as bad for growth
prospects and risky asset valuations as high inflation is; both problems
breed instability. Just recall the 1930s and 2008 experiences, or Japan in the
1990s. As Figure 8.10 showed, historical P/E10 ratios have a sombrero
shape 
when 
scatterplotted 
in 
buckets 
on 
the 
inflation 
level. 
Market
valuations are especially high amidst low, but positive, stable inflation.

Real estate. Data are limited but many studies suggest that direct real
estate holdings provide a good hedge against inflation over the long run.
Conceptually, this makes sense because rental yields are a key part of real
estate return and rents form a roughly constant share of renters’ income,
which 
changes 
with 
inflation. 
Moreover, 
operationally, 
rents 
can 
be
adjusted 
to 
reflect 
changing 
inflation. 
Other 
real 
assets, 
such 
as
infrastructure, 
timber, 
and 
farmland 
are 
thought 
to 
have 
an 
inflation-
hedging ability similar to that of directly owned real estate.

Evidence on inflation and housing booms is complex: one major boom
occurred in a high-inflation environment in the 1970s and another in a low-
inflation environment in the 2000s. Piazzesi–Schneider (2008) argue that
both 
low 
and 
high 
inflation 
can 
produce 
housing 
booms, 
due 
to
disagreement about inflation and real rates between rational and confused
or inflation-illusioned investors. Confused households perceived high real
rates in the 1970s while rational ones saw low real rates and bought houses
by borrowing as much as they could; in the 2000s the reverse occurred.
Wallison 
(2009) 
argues 
that 
the 
housing 
boom 
of 
the 
2000s 
was
government-program-driven (at least in the United States) and would not
have occurred if mortgage agencies had not been directed to aggressively
lower their creditworthiness standards.

Oil, gold, and other commodities. Many commodities are reasonable
inflation hedges but energy even benefits from inflation (in part because
rising 
energy 
prices 
are, 
in 
many 
circumstances, 
the 
main 
cause 
of
inflation). It is interesting that among major commodities in recent decades,
oil has the best inflation-hedging ability, highest average backwardation,
and highest average return. Too much of a good thing. It is probably no
accident that all these benefits coincide—it is likely an artifact of two
events, the oil embargo and supply crises of the 1970s and demand squeeze
driving oil prices skyward in the mid-2000s. Gold earned its highest returns
around 
the 
1979–1980 
inflation 
rate 
peak 
but 
also 
fared 
well 
under
disinflationary conditions in recent years. The role of gold as a safe haven
against financial turmoil, low real yields (little opportunity cost), dollar
weakness, and lack of attractive alternative currencies may explain gold’s
recent success better than its role as an inflation hedge. Unlike other
commodities, gold can perform well even under deflationary conditions;
empirically, 
inflation 
uncertainty 
or 
disagreement 
predicts 
future 
gold
returns better than the inflation level does.

Currencies. High inflation also undermines currencies in the short term
because they lose their international purchasing power. As an exception, if
the central bank is sufficiently credible that markets view any rise in
inflation as temporary and expect it to trigger policy tightening, higher
inflation may result in short-term currency appreciation. Purchasing power
parity does not hold in the short term but works reasonably well over long
horizons. In the case of hyperinflation, in particular, exchange rate changes
tend to offset persistent cross-country inflation differentials.

Deflation—topical and scary

It is important to distinguish between disinflation (which typically helps
risky assets) and deflation (which can be destructive). We do not have much
data on deflationary environments but what we have looks scary (the U.S.
in the 1930s, Japan more recently). Deflation tends to hurt most asset
classes, so it is difficult to hedge against. Nominal government bonds are
the most obvious hedges—but even they may be less than fully effective if
a 
deflationary 
recession 
raises 
market 
concerns 
about 
sovereign
creditworthiness.

Unfortunately, the current environment resembles classic debt deflation
(which is preceded by excessive debt accumulation and characterized by
de-leveraging) more than it does the benign deflation experience of the late
19th 
century, 
when 
stock 
prices, 
real 
output, 
and 
incomes 
all 
rose
tremendously. In the latter case, deflation was caused by technological
improvement (increased productivity) during a period when money supply
growth was severely constrained by use of the gold standard.

17.4 TIME-VARYING INFLATION PREMIUM

Clearly, the IRP has not been constant over time. I outlined in Chapter 9
and in this chapter my view that the IRP varies broadly with inflation level
and inflation uncertainty. Moreover, the IRP is higher when high-inflation
episodes coincide with recessions. Both arguments justified a high IRP in
the 1970s and 1980s. If stock–bond correlations are positive at the same
time, equities, too, can have a meaningful IRP. In contrast, the past decade
has seen conditions under which the IRP is negligible: inflation has been
low and stable, and, if anything, bad times have coincided with deflation
scares. However, it is easy to see scenarios in the coming decade where
inflation expectations become unhinged again and bring back a significant
IRP, perhaps accompanied by a sovereign default premium.

[2] It is important to stress that the presence (even once) or absence of

example, postwar hyperinflation in Japan destroyed wealth much more
effectively than the 1930s’ Great Depression in the U.S. or the 1990s’ lost
decade in Japan. Japanese equities lost nearly all of their value within a year
but managed to recover postwar losses by 1960. For nominal assets the
losses were more permanent.

[3] Empirical Taylor rules show that inflation-targeting central banks also
apply countercyclic demand management, even if they don’t have the Fed’s
dual mandate on inflation and employment. In an expectations-augmented
Phillips curve model, wage and price rigidities justify some real effects
from monetary policy, albeit temporary. Eventually, inflation expectations
adjust 
and 
in 
the 
long

only 
raises 
inflation.
Friedman’s quote about inflation being always a monetary phenomenon is
true in the sense that sustained inflation needs central bank accommodation
(money 
supply

sustainably

money

However, the unstable relationship between monetary growth and inflation
has made monetary indicators unreliable policy tools. Close cousins to
monetary indicators are making a comeback in financial conditions indices
that include money and credit aggregates. Learning from recent boom–bust
cycles, some policymakers show renewed interest in indicators beyond
consumer price inflation. Notably, they appear more willing to consider
“leaning against the wind” policies when asset prices and credit growth
seem unsustainably high, in part to promote financial stability and to resist
procyclicality.
