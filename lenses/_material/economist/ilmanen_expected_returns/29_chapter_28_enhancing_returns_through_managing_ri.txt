Enhancing returns through managing risks, horizon, skill, and
costs

• This chapter reviews four paths for improving portfolio
performance and drills into each of them.
• Risks. The simple way to boost long-run returns is to raise
the weight of riskier assets in the portfolio. The more complex but
better rewarding approach requires leverage. Within each asset
class, buying the riskiest assets almost invariably gives worse
long-run returns than levering up lower volatility assets. Even
across asset classes, investors can reduce portfolio volatility by
smart diversification and then convert improved risk-adjusted
returns into higher raw returns by applying moderate leverage.
• Horizon. Investors with a long time horizon can exploit it
by over-allocating to illiquid asset classes and reaping related
illiquidity premia. Other possibilities include contrarian market
timing and opportunistic reinsurance underwriting.
• Skill. Active investors may try to add value through skillful
security selection and market timing, either in house or externally.
Even when markets are not fully efficient, they are competitive
enough to make the task challenging. Building an active
management culture and resources internally is not easy, nor is it
easy to identify consistent outperformers among external
managers.
• Costs. Reducing fees and other implementation costs is
often the easiest way to enhance net returns. It is harder to decide
on the allocation between (cheap, abundant) beta products and
(costly, scarce) alpha, because the higher fees and trading costs in
actively managed investments are sometimes worth it. Relative
weights should depend on investor views regarding market
efficiency and one’s own skills at active investing or manager
selection.
• After an overview, this chapter drills into each path and
also contains essays on related topics such as rebalancing and
diversification return, looking beyond the Sharpe ratio, and time
diversification.

28.1.2 Exploiting a long horizon

Investors with a long horizon do not need short-term liquidity. If the market
rewards illiquid assets with an illiquidity premium, it makes sense that
long-horizon investors over-allocate to such assets. These include private
equity and other alternative asset classes as well as certain pockets of
traditional asset classes (small-cap stocks, corporate bonds). However, it is
folly to expect illiquidity premia to be constant over time or to be realized
when extra money happens to be needed. Many investors raised their
allocations to illiquid asset classes in 2005–2007 just when their valuations
were high and prospective premia negligible.

More generally, long-horizon investors should be better able to take
advantage of time-varying expected returns by contrarian market timing.
The combination of a long horizon and deep pockets gives some large
sovereign 
wealth 
funds 
(SWFs) 
a 
natural 
edge 
in 
being 
the 
liquidity
provider 
within 
major 
asset 
classes 
when 
assets 
are 
distressed. 
These
investors 
could 
also 
follow 
Warren 
Buffett 
into 
some 
unconventional
strategies where patience and deep pockets help. Examples: Opportunistic
entries into reinsurance underwriting after recent catastrophes have made
reinsurance 
capital 
scarce

prospective 
rewards 
for
underwriting. 
The

SWFs 
made 
into 
financial

It

Institutional and individual horizons tend to get shorter during financial

“long-horizon wannabes”, a situation that pushed ex ante liquidity premia to
negligible levels. During the crisis these premia shot up, which to a rational
investor is an indicator to hold on to their investments and maybe even
exploit new buying opportunities. Yet even some endowments and pension
plans—the prototypical long-term investors—felt obliged (or were obliged
by 
cash 
flow

to 
sell 
illiquid 
holdings 
into 
unreceptive

Investors increasingly appreciate the cost of illiquidity (and the risk in
assets

covary

deteriorating

conditions). It is becoming more common to design contracts with external
managers in which the end-investor gets appropriately compensated for
lockups and other undesirable terms and accepts higher fees for easy
liquidity terms. However, the contracted liquidity terms should be realistic,
or managers may honor end-investor redemption calls by selling assets at

28.1.3 Benefiting from skill

Active investors can try to add value through skillful security selection and
market timing, sector timing, or style timing. Active investing may be done
internally or externally, neither of which is easy in competitive financial
markets. Developing sufficient skill in house and maintaining the talent is a
major challenge, but so is identifying consistent alpha producers among
external managers.

One 
key 
distinction 
is 
between 
discretionary 
and 
systematic 
styles.
Discretionary managers tend to focus on specific companies, industries, or
other 
individual 
securities 
(say, 
commodity 
futures) 
and 
are 
usually
specialists. Systematic 
managers emphasize value-added through broad
diversification (applying key ideas such as value and momentum in many
markets), smart portfolio construction and risk management, and cost-
effective implementation. These activities also require skill, but according
to some definitions only discretionary managers can provide pure alpha;
anything that can be written down is, at best, alternative beta.

Skill-based investing is increasingly associated with HFs. The idea is that
alpha is best reaped by well-motivated unconstrained managers using some
combination of leverage, long–short investing, and derivatives. Yet, to beat
high-fee structures, you need some expertise or luck to pick top-half
managers. 
Success 
at 
HF 
selection 
is 
made 
more 
plausible 
because
alternative 
managers 
exhibit 
wider 
performance 
dispersion 
as 
well 
as
greater 
performance 
persistence 
and 
predictability 
than 
traditional
managers.

A common view is that skill is best applied in illiquid asset classes and in
market segments where mispricings are harder to arbitrage and thus they
survive longer. A counterview is that it is easier to identify skill vs. luck in
liquid assets with frequent performance measurement. With illiquid assets,
effective sample sizes will be tiny. Moreover, if you reassess a manager’s
skill over time, there will be times (say in 2008) when you may want to get
out but you have no flexibility to do so.

28.1.4 Cost control

Passive investing minimizes trading costs. However, some costs are worth
paying. Buying an equity index fund costs more than investing in a bank
deposit, but the equity premium should make the extra cost worthwhile in
the long run. Value-oriented non-market-cap weighting involves higher
turnover and fees than market cap indices, but proponents of fundamental
indexation argue that long-run net returns will be better. Beta products are
cheap, while scarcity makes alpha costly; yet it does not automatically
follow that alpha allocations should be avoided.

Minimizing costs is not always smart; being cost-effective and avoiding
wasteful expense is.

In assessing tradeoffs, such as the relative weights of active and passive
(alpha 
and 
beta) 
products, 
reasonable 
answers 
vary 
across 
investors.
Clearly, a retail investor with high trading costs and few natural edges is
almost 
always 
better 
off 
with 
low-cost 
beta 
products. 
In 
contrast, 
a
resource-rich institution with low trading costs has a better chance at
succeeding in active management. (A caveat: Large investors often suffer
from higher market impact costs.)

In general, investors should allocate more to alpha products the less they
believe in market efficiency and the more they believe in their own skills
(in active investing or manager selection). Skepticism is healthy. It should
be remembered that most active long-only managers underperform their
benchmarks after fees and that retail investors fare even worse. The record
of HF managers, as a group, looks better but it is hard to fully adjust for the
impact of voluntary reporting biases and various risks. To end ahead after
fees, you must be able to identify the better managers, which is a challenge.

Alternative managers charge pricey fees, especially if they can boast a
good track record. However, trying to beat the markets in-house “on the
cheap”—with poorly resourced and incentivized internal managers—may
be even more costly. To be cost-effective, some end-investors go for an
alpha–beta barbell (combining HFs and passive market indices), while
others try to save costs by buying into alternative beta products rather than
actual HFs.

Investors should look beyond visible costs even with passive strategies: if
there is no cost advantage in staple indices, you should favor customized
benchmarks to avoid synchronous portfolio adjustments with big index
players. For example, the S&P 500 index loses 20 bp to 30 bp each year due
to the market impact of concentrated buying and selling around index
additions and deletions. Meanwhile, the way that S&P GSCI Commodity
index owners synchronously roll into new futures contracts each month
causes a predictable temporary supply–demand imbalance that hurts these
index investors.

28.2.1 Monetized risk reduction via leverage

Many 
retail 
investor 
portfolios 
are 
insufficiently 
diversified, 
perhaps
consisting 
of 
only 
a 
few 
stocks 
and 
having 
plenty 
of 
diversifiable,
unrewarded risk. It turns out that typical institutional portfolios are also
poorly diversified, but not from holding too small a number of stocks; the
reason is that a 60/40 nominal stock/bond allocation implies over 95% of
the risk budget (portfolio volatility) coming from the equity component.
Such concentrated risk taking is inefficient; a more balanced risk budget
would give higher risk-adjusted returns. One way to improve the portfolio’s
SR is to lever up the bond component to a volatility level similar to that of
the equity component. This approach is often called risk parity investing.

Table 28.1 shows that an equal volatility stock/bond portfolio (i.e., 50/50
volatility weights for stocks and bonds) has a tad higher Sharpe ratio (0.40
vs. 0.38) and a clearly lower correlation with the equity market (0.76 vs.
0.96) than an equal nominal weight stock/bond portfolio. All position sizes
can be scaled up or down to reach any desired portfolio volatility target.
Investors who accept more leverage can select a higher return for equity-
type volatility (the last column in the table) instead of lower volatility for an
equity-type expected return. Empirical results are similar whether I use “in-
sample” or (trailing) “out-of-sample” volatilities.

The Sharpe ratio improvement in this example is not too compelling,
partly because the stock–bond correlation was still positive over this 40-
year window. Gains from risk parity investing would rise with a lower
stock–bond correlation, lower relative volatility between bonds and stocks,
and a higher relative Sharpe ratio between them. Broadening into several
moderately correlated asset classes would improve diversification and the
portfolio’s SR faster, even if the generally high correlations of most assets
with equities limit the benefits.

28.2.2 Rebalancing and “diversification return”

Rebalancing asset classes toward target weights is a common risk control
approach. 
Somewhat 
miraculously, 
under 
relatively 
undemanding
assumptions (but not in all possible scenarios), rebalancing also boosts a
portfolio’s long-run compound return. In most cases this effect is quite
mild, but it is interesting that this return enhancement does not require
leverage.

Market moves can make asset class weights drift from their target
weights—say, a 60/40 stock/bond allocation. Rebalancing refers to trading
activity that counters such a drift. It involves buying asset classes that
recently underperformed and selling recent outperformers. Rebalancing
may be calendar based or trigger based, and a rebalancing rule may push
asset weights all the way back to the target or only to the edge of a “no-
trading band”.

It makes intuitive sense that rebalancing is profitable if asset class returns
are mean-reverting (at frequencies well synchronized with rebalancing
activity). 
More 
surprisingly, 
rebalancing 
turns 
out 
to 
boost 
long-run
compound returns even if asset returns exhibit no mean reversion tendency.
My 
analysis 
is 
mainly 
based 
on 
three 
papers: 
Booth–Fama 
(1992),
Bernstein–Wilkinson (1997), and Erb–Harvey (2006).

Here are the key ideas behind how this gain arises in a world with zero-
autocorrelated 
returns 
(neither 
mean 
reversion 
nor 
momentum 
bias 
in
returns, and asset prices follow a random walk):
• The technical reason can be traced to a distinction between
arithmetic and geometric means. An asset’s or a portfolio’s geometric
mean (GM) (i.e., its compound return) depends both on its arithmetic
mean 
(AM) 
and 
its 
variance. 
Higher 
volatility 
erodes 
compound
returns and thus wealth accumulation over time.
• Diversification reduces a portfolio’s variance and thus boosts its
GM, given constant AMs. Diversification return (DR) is the excess of
a portfolio’s GM over its constituents’ weighted average GM.
• Rebalancing to constant weights is needed to earn the DR.
Rebalancing ensures diversification—by not letting one asset class
grow to dominate the portfolio.

Box 28.1. Arithmetic and geometric means

As noted in Section 4.3, the arithmetic mean of a series is always
higher than the geometric mean (AM > GM) except when there is zero
volatility (AM = GM). A simple Taylor series expansion shows that a
good approximation is:

GM ≈ AM—Variance/2.

There has been an ongoing debate in financial circles for decades as
to which measure is more appropriate for assessing long-term expected
returns and terminal wealth. Textbooks have traditionally sided with
AM, while practitioners often prefer GM, perhaps because the latter is
correct for measuring realized compound return.

The AM–GM distinction is related to the difference between mean
and median. Long-horizon cumulative asset returns are highly skewed:
expected returns are unbounded from above—asset prices can rise
hundreds of percent or more—but have a bounded downside as they
can “only” lose—100%. Due to positive skewness caused by a few
extremely positive outcomes, the mathematical expected return (AM)
can be a very atypical outcome. While AMs overstate the long-run
future return investors are likely to realize, GMs are more like medians
that give more realistic forecasts for long-horizon investors. In the
presence of estimation errors, an unbiased estimate of future portfolio
value depends more on the GM the longer the time horizon (for a
given historical sample length).

The GM ≈ AM—Half the variance approximation holds for a single
asset and for a portfolio, including a market index. For example, if
equities have a long-run annualized volatility of 20%, variance is
20%
2
= 4%. Thus, the expected variance drain (the gap between AM
and GM) is 2%. (Actual data on the annual returns of U.S. large-cap
stocks between 1926 and 2006 agree: the AM was 12.3%, GM 10.4%,
and volatility 20%.) For bond indices with annual return volatility of
6% to 7%, the variance drain is only about 0.2%.

Because the geometric mean reflects both the arithmetic mean and
variance, some academics and practitioners have long recommended
the multi-period strategy of maximizing the portfolio’s expected
compound return as an alternative to mean variance optimization.

“Turning water into wine”

Here is an example of diversification return from Erb–Harvey (2006): an
annually rebalanced portfolio consisting of 50% heating oil and 50% stocks
had an excess return (GM) of 11.0% over a decade, much higher than either
component. Heating oil had an excess return (GM) of 8.2% but an AM near
18%. Stocks had an excess return (GM) of 6.8% but an AM near 9%.
Diversification return was about 3.5%, thanks to variance reduction.

Diversification reduces the variance drain and thereby boosts portfolio
geometric means

A 
portfolio’s 
return 
equals 
the 
(weighted) 
average 
AM 
return 
of 
its
constituents whereas variances are not additive unless constituents are
perfectly correlated. Diversification is the rare free lunch available for all
investors: we can reduce portfolio volatility without reducing its AM. This
volatility reduction improves the portfolio’s GM by reducing its variance
drain. It follows that the GM of a portfolio exceeds the average GM of its
constituents. The difference is the DR. That is, investors can raise their
portfolio GM by identifying a new investment that is uncorrelated with the
original portfolio and has comparable return. This gain (DR) is proportional
to half the new asset’s variance. I stress that diversification boosts the GM
but not the AM. Yet, since diversification reduces volatility, investors who
can lever up their portfolio back to the original volatility level can also
enhance their AM.

Importance of rebalancing
In practice, earning the DR requires rebalancing to some constant weights
(not necessarily equal). Rebalancing reduces the weights of outperforming
assets. If asset weights are allowed to drift significantly over time, the most
successful 
assets 
over 
long 
samples 
may 
come 
to 
dominate 
portfolio
weights and dilute any diversification effects.

Erb and Harvey stress that the DR really reflects two features: the variance
drain and rebalancing. They give the label “covariance drag” to the negative
impact on GM of not rebalancing; by definition, this drag is zero for
continuously 
rebalanced 
strategies. 
In 
practice, 
unrebalanced 
strategies
(with “let-it-run” weights following some initial weighting) often have a
near-zero DR. The variance reduction benefit and covariance drag roughly
offset each other, thereby making the portfolio GM roughly equal to the
constituents’ average GM. This is also true for a market-weighted portfolio,
which is just one example of an unrebalanced strategy.

Factors that drive diversification return

The DR is especially large for portfolios with high asset volatility and low
correlations. Because the constituents of typical equity portfolios (or bond
portfolios) tend to be relatively highly correlated, their DR is modest.
Moreover, since typical equity and bond portfolios are rarely rebalanced to
constant weights, their covariance drag likely offsets the variance drain
effect. When rebalancing asset allocation weights between equity and bond
portfolios (and other asset classes), the DR should be higher, given the low
correlations between asset classes.

As 
a 
good 
approximation, 
expected 
equally

proportional to average variance, and (3) is negatively proportional to the

Extension 1 Relatively similar long-run expected returns across assets favor
rebalancing

The analysis of Bernstein–Wilkinson (1997) is more general than that of
Erb and Harvey and highlights the role of expected return differences across
assets better. First, the intuition: If assets in the portfolio have persistently
different expected returns, rebalancing involves gradual selling of high-
return assets. A no-rebalancing strategy would let the highest return asset
eventually 
grow 
to 
constitute 
the 
entire 
portfolio 
and 
thus 
make 
the
portfolio’s expected return equal the highest returning asset’s expected
return. Given sufficiently large expected return differences across assets, the
benefit from this effect must exceed any diversification benefit maintained
via rebalancing.

This study shows that the GM gap between rebalanced and unrebalanced
portfolios can be proxied by two terms:
• the first term favors the unrebalanced portfolio; it is large when
GMs are highly dispersed across assets and is zero when all assets
have equal GMs—in line with the Bernstein–Wilkinson generalization;
• the second term favors the rebalanced portfolio; it is large when
individual assets have large variances and low/negative correlations—
in line with Erb and Harvey’s analysis.

Empirical exercises suggest that the latter term dominates. Only when GM
differences are very wide (say, rebalancing between low-yielding Treasury
bills 
and 
high-return 
assets) 
does 
the 
net 
result 
favor 
unrebalanced
portfolios.

A close cousin: Volatility targeting

Instead of targeting certain nominal weights, leveraged investors often

assets 
whose 
unlevered 
standalone

differ 
hugely. 
HFs

Assigning 
equal 
volatilities 
to 
an 
active 
portfolio’s 
risk

the near term than returns are. Simply targeting a constant volatility often
improves long-run returns compared with a strategy of maintaining constant

28.2.3 Risk adjustment: Looking beyond the Sharpe ratio

The SR is by far the most popular measure of risk-adjusted return for
absolute return managers and investment strategies. It is rightly viewed as a
tremendously useful tool for investment analysis and portfolio construction.
Indeed, investors often make SR maximization the key objective for their
portfolio [1].

Yet, the SR has its limitations, mirroring those of volatility as a risk
measure for a portfolio. The world is too complex for any single number to
capture its 
multi-faceted 
risks. 
Simply put, not all 
volatility 
is equal.
Reasonable investors may accept a lower portfolio SR in exchange for
improving other portfolio characteristics. I stress four arguments that justify
looking beyond volatility as the risk measure and thus beyond SR as the
performance measure:
• losses in bad times hurt more than bull market losses—thus
protecting the portfolio in bad times is essential;
• large losses are particularly disliked—thus investors should
differentiate between upside and downside volatility;
• illiquid assets warrant higher SRs as a result of illiquidity
premia and understated volatilities;
• 
adverse 
tail 
events 
are 
made 
more 
likely 
by 
complexity,
operational risk, and other unquantifiable features.

Each argument implies a tradeoff between the SR and some other portfolio
attribute. This subsection brings together the many dimensions of the
problem and provides empirical examples.

Tradeoff #1 Timing of losses

The SR does not care whether gains or losses occur under good or bad
economic conditions. Most investors do. For a given SR, they prefer an
investment that made money in 2008 over one that made money in 2006.
The year 2008 was the annus horribilis for today’s investors. Besides re-
acquiring 
general 
respect 
for 
risk 
in 
2008, 
investors 
re-learned 
to
differentiate between volatility sources:
• Nearly all risky and illiquid assets, as well as positive-carry
strategies 
and 
absolute 
return 
managers, 
turned 
out 
to 
be 
bad
diversifiers: they became even more positively 
correlated 
than 
in
normal times.
• Good diversifiers were rare. The scarcity of safe haven assets
makes them especially valuable, as they provide diversification when
it is most needed. Trend-following strategies were among the few safe
havens, besides government bonds.

These patterns are not unique to 2008. We can use two contrasting trading
styles—carry seeking and trend following—as illustrations of bad and good
diversifiers. This example has broader applicability because many fund
managers and HF style indices are well characterized by their exposure to
either style (or to both). Figure 28.1 highlights the opposite directional risks
of

Tradeoff #2 Return asymmetry

Volatility treats gains and losses equally. Investors of course like gains and
dislike losses—especially large losses (negative skewness). When it comes
to return asymmetry, the trend proxy has greater upside volatility than
downside volatility while the opposite is true for the carry proxy (and most
risky 
assets). 
Moreover, 
the 
trend 
proxy 
has 
much 
smaller 
maximum
drawdown and better skewness characteristics (where positive skewness is
preferred to negative) than the carry proxy.

Both style proxies have been profitable in recent decades, with appealing
long-run paper SRs near one [3]. When we look beyond the SR, the trend
style 
is 
clearly 
the 
more 
benign 
source 
of 
return, 
given 
its 
superior
diversification 
ability 
and 
positive 
skew. 
In 
contrast, 
the 
carry 
style
resembles “selling lottery tickets that pay off in bad times”, combining
negative skewness with toxic timing of losses—besides being vulnerable to
abuse by unskilled managers.

Tradeoff #3 Liquidity

The SR ignores liquidity considerations, but we know that illiquid assets
have artificially smooth returns and thus overstated SRs. Moreover, illiquid
assets 
should 
offer 
some 
compensation 
(illiquidity 
premium) 
for 
their
higher trading costs and lesser flexibility as well as for their tendency to
underperform during liquidity droughts.
Both considerations imply that investors should require higher measured
SRs for illiquid asset classes than for liquid asset classes. Recall that Figure
2.2 showed a positive relation across asset classes between our illiquidity
estimates and long-run SRs. It is possible, however, that the desire of many
investors 
to 
avoid 
mark-to-market 
volatility 
makes 
less 
liquid 
assets
overpriced, reducing the reward for illiquidity.

Tradeoff #4 Unquantifiable tail risks

Some investors may also trade off the SR against complexity, opacity,
novelty, 
funding 
leverage, 
overcrowding, 
or 
operational 
risk. 
These
attributes 
are 
less 
quantifiable 
than 
the 
portfolio’s 
diversification 
or
asymmetry characteristics but they all contribute to tail risks. Being simpler
and more robust, more transparent and better tested, not being hostage to a
lender’s whims or to sharp liquidations, and not being subject to blatant
operational risks are all factors that arguably make tail events less likely.
Investors may focus too much on measurable “normal times” risks and too
little on unquantifiable risks. The latter often dominate tail events and
disproportionately occur in bad times. Operational risks (errors and fraud)
are a good example; the SR of Madoff’s track record was hard to beat but it
came with huge operational risk.

Conclusions

The portfolio SR is a good starting point but it needs to be supplemented
with other portfolio attributes. All of the desirable attributes discussed
above may be worth some SR sacrifice. However, no single risk-adjusted
return measure can capture them all, and many of these tradeoffs can only
be assessed in a qualitative fashion. Multiple performance metrics are
needed, given the multi-dimensional nature of the problem.

28.2.4 Smart risk taking and portfolio construction

There now follow some intuitive rules of thumb for smart investing: a
recipe 
for 
optimal 
diversification 
and 
the 
“fundamental 
law 
of 
active
management”.

First, here is a recipe for smart portfolio construction, which sums up
mean variance optimization in a nutshell: allocate equal volatility to each
asset class (or return source) in a portfolio, unless some assets’ exceptional
SRs 
or 
diversification 
abilities 
justify 
deviating 
from 
equal 
volatility
weightings. Asset classes with high SRs or with negative correlations (vs.
the rest of the portfolio) should be overweighted until the portfolio SR can
no longer be improved.
• If assets are equally attractive in terms of expected returns and
diversification abilities, they deserve equal volatility shares in an
optimal 
portfolio. 
Allocating 
equal 
volatility 
shares 
maximizes
diversification if all assets have similar diversification abilities.
• Equal volatility portfolios ignore information about expected
returns—as 
do 
minimum 
variance 
or 
maximum 
diversification
portfolios. These approaches implicitly assume that all assets have the
same reward–risk ratio.
• Since this recipe loosely mimics mean variance optimization, it
shares any shortcomings of the mean variance framework, such as
ignoring liquidity considerations.

The hugely influential fundamental law of active management, discovered
by Richard Grinold, states that, under certain simplifying assumptions, a
portfolio’s 
risk-adjusted 
return 
in 
excess 
of 
its 
benchmark 
(i.e., 
the
portfolio’s information ratio or IR), reflects skill and breadth:
where the information coefficient, IC, is the amount of investment skill per
trade, 
measuring 
the 
investor’s 
forecasting 
ability 
by 
the 
correlation
between 
predictions 
and 
realizations; 
and 
breadth 
is 
the 
number 
of
independent trades (i.e., trades not using the same information) per year,
representing diversification opportunities. (Note that the IR is the part of the
SR due to active management against a benchmark, the rest is due to
variation in the returns of the benchmark. If the portfolio’s benchmark has
zero volatility, then IR = SR.)
The breadth concept underscores the shortcomings of market-timing
strategies where few independent views are taken each year. Breadth can be
improved 
by 
selecting 
portfolios 
from 
a 
large 
set 
of 
securities, 
by
combining many strategies, or by taking views more frequently. That is, for
a single strategy, updating a view with a given predictive correlation (IC) on
a weekly basis boosts the SR or IR about seven times (
) more than
updating a view with the same correlation on an annual basis. Apparently, if
you market-time, you should change your views often (as long as your
views have a reasonably high probability of being correct). In reality it is
harder to make accurate forecasts every week than once a year; moreover,
higher trading costs may fully offset any benefits of fast-moving signals.

It seems likely that widening breadth comes at the expense of lesser skill.
Breadth also assumes that trades are uncorrelated, while in reality many
trades tend to use the same information, making them positively correlated.
Highly positively correlated trades are poor diversifiers, while negatively
correlated trades are excellent diversifiers. The latter are rare but are often
worth adding to the portfolio even if they (modestly) reduce its expected
returns. Examples of pairs of such super-diversifiers include pro-growth
equities and anti-growth Treasuries, the value or contrarian style and the
momentum style, and carry and trend styles.

Extensions to the fundamental law incorporate constraints, trading costs,
or 
illiquidity 
; 
allowing 
correlations 
between 
forecasts 
and 
assets;
recognizing 
the 
uncertainty 
of 
inputs, 
or 
relaxing 
other 
underlying
assumptions such as normally distributed asset returns or mean variance
preferences:
• When assets and/or strategies have symmetrically distributed
returns, volatility and downside risk measures are interchangeable.
When not, it is useful to track portfolio skewness or downside risk
measures as well as overall volatility. For many traders, stop-loss
limits and other risk management rules are essential tools, but the use
of risk control techniques may reduce expected returns.
• Robust approaches and simple measures bring more stable
performance 
in 
a 
world 
where 
non-normalities, 
outliers 
and 
rare
events, 
estimation 
errors, 
and 
time-varying 
relations 
abound.
Moreover, we rarely find that complex approaches really improve real-
world performance, though this is naturally a topic of debate among
quants.

Diversification of course has limitations, one of which is the tendency for
correlations to approach one during crises [4]. Many good traders and
absolute return managers distinguish themselves by managing downside
risk instead of just relying on diversification. They use options, stop-loss
rules, and innovative ways to buy tail risk insurance to limit the downside.
Absolute 
return 
managers 
often 
emphasize 
that 
risk 
management 
and
avoidance of large drawdowns is key, first, to their survival and, second, to
their good long-run compound returns.

28.3 INVESTMENT HORIZON

Exploiting a long horizon

Long-horizon investors are well suited to capturing positive illiquidity
premia, term premia, and even other risk premia (as they can tolerate such
risks better than the average investor in the market). True long-horizon
investors are a minority in the marketplace. Indeed, the relative share of
short-horizon and long-horizon investors in the market should influence the
size of such premia. Arguably, the share of both true and wannabe long-
horizon investors grew rapidly in the mid-2000s, a situation that contributed
to 
excessive 
narrowing 
of 
these 
premia 
in 
2005–2007. 
Long-horizon
investors may not only have a natural edge in generally holding illiquid and
risky assets but also in being opportunistic buyers and liquidity providers
whenever the market’s risk aversion and liquidity needs are especially high
[5].

Box 28.2. Compound returns

A probably apocryphal quote has Einstein calling compound interest
the eighth wonder of the world. Table 28.2 illustrates the power of
compounding. Over 50 years, an investment earning a 4% annual
return grows sevenfold. If a 10% return can be achieved, it grows 117-
fold! Any investment marketer worth her salt knows that showing final
wealth over a multi-decade window is much more impressive than
showing annualized return. To really benefit from compounding, an
investor needs time, return, and re-investing. The last two rows of
Table 28.2 show that 111 of the 117 final dollars were due to re-
investment return.
The best known story of compound interest is the legend of Indians
selling the island of Manhattan in 1626 to a Dutch merchant for $24
worth of glass beads. Had these Indians invested and earned a
compound return near 6% for 380 years, their stake would have grown
to about $100 billion with which they could have bought back a good
chunk of Manhattan real estate in 2006—with improvements. Had the
Indians managed to compound their wealth at an 8% rate, the price of
the beads would have grown into $120 trillion—close to the best
current estimate of all financial wealth in the world.

Of course, nobody could ever compound wealth over centuries at
6%, let alone at 8%. Even if the U.S. equity market had offered close
to an 8% nominal compound return over the past 200 years, taxes,
fees, and trading costs would have chipped away a decent chunk of
this return. Just as importantly or more so, no family or institution has
had the patience to keep re-investing for centuries without redemptions
and spending.

In Table 28.2 I underline two values near 2. They are examples of
the convenient “rule of 72” which says that the number of years in
which you double your money is approximated by the number 72
divided by the percentage rate of return. Doubling your money takes
72 years at 1 %, 16 years at 4%, about 10 years at 7%, and about 7
years at 10%.

The investment horizon can be intimately tied to an institution’s liabilities,
explicit or implicit. For many pension funds and life insurance companies,
long-dated (nominal or real) liabilities imply that long-dated (nominal or
real) bonds are effectively riskless assets. Indeed, these institutions’ demand
for long-dated bonds has contributed to the flatness or inversion of the back
end of many yield curves. In many real-world cases, the investment horizon
is uncertain (and the perceived horizon is often positively related to market
conditions 
and 
investor 
risk

Banks, 
central 
banks, 
and
corporations tend to have a short horizon, while customer deposits, FX
reserves, and excess cash tend to be held for long periods. These institutions
need to be prepared to satisfy sudden cash demands (such as are caused by
a bank run, currency crisis, or various corporate expenses). Endowments,
foundations, and sovereign wealth funds are closest to having permanent
capital, but the first two have recurring spending needs each year, whereas
the sovereign wealth funds of some commodity-rich countries can expect
their net inflows to grow for another decade and net outflows to start only
in the distant future.

Time diversification

Time diversification—the idea that stock market investing becomes less
risky with a longer horizon—is a contentious issue. Jeremy Siegel, who
wrote several editions of a book entitled Stocks for the Long Run, has
studied over two centuries of U.S. equity market returns and publicized the
empirical fact that, although short-term returns are nearly as likely to be
down as up, the probability of losing money over a 20-year window has
been 
negligible. 
Realized 
variances 
of 
annualized 
returns 
over 
long
horizons are significantly lower than those over short horizons. These
results could reflect long-run mean reversion and/or exceptionally benign
market experience in the U.S., but they may be more generally true.

Indeed, 
it 
is 
a 
mathematical 
fact 
that 
if 
stock 
returns 
have 
zero
autocorrelation (i.e., if they are a random walk) the volatility of average
annual returns falls with a longer horizon, and if stock returns are a random
walk with upward drift (i.e., positive expected return) the likelihood of
negative cumulative returns falls with a longer horizon. Critics counter that
the volatility of cumulative returns and the magnitude of potential losses
increases with the time horizon.

Kritzman (2000) reminds us of Paul Samuelson’s mathematical result
that under certain conditions expected utility maximizers should hold a
constant share of their wealth in risky assets irrespective of their investment
horizon. Merely having a longer horizon does not make investors more risk
tolerant as long as they have constant relative risk aversion, asset returns
follow a random walk, and future wealth depends only on investment
returns—not on human capital. Relaxing any of these assumptions can
justify a greater weight in risky assets for long-horizon investors. Thus
stock market investing can be safer for them:
(i) if they exhibit risk aversion that is less wealth dependent than
that of typical investors (whose risk aversion may increase sharply
when they become poorer and approach their subsistence level); or
(ii) if stock returns are mean-reverting (losses over one period are
more likely to be offset by higher returns in the future, thereby
reducing multi-year volatility); or
(iii) if they are young with flexible and plentiful human capital
(first, in case equity returns disappoint, they can offset the impact by
working a little harder to supplement their labor income; second, the
mere fact that human capital is a larger share of their total wealth when
they are young suggests that they can take more risk with their
financial wealth).

One strand of the debate over time diversification studies the preference-
free environment of option pricing; some argue that the cost of insurance
against declines in the market rises with the length of the time horizon,
others that the cost of such insurance declines on a per annum basis.

New research adds a powerful argument against time diversification.
Pastor and Stambaugh stress the uncertainty about mean returns, which is a
more 
important 
long-term 
risk 
than 
the 
classic 
measure 
(short-term
volatility around the mean). Mean stock market return has been near 10% in
the past, but this is only an estimate, and the future mean will almost
certainly be different. When uncertainty about the true mean (and other
parameters) is included, long-horizon investors face more volatility than
short-horizon investors.

Box 28.3. Retail investors and the time dimension

Lifecycle investing. As noted above, young people can more easily
compensate for poor realized market returns with greater work effort;
therefore young people can hold a larger share of their wealth in
stocks. Many financial advisors use the rule of thumb “percentage in
bonds and cash equals your age”. This idea is at the heart of so-called
target date funds. While the proximate goal is to ensure sufficient
retirement income, the broader goal is consumption smoothing over
the lifecycle. Others emphasize the importance of guaranteeing some
minimum level of inflation-protected pension income by purchasing
real lifetime annuities. Pension investment decisions are further
complicated by the uncertainty of personal health costs and longevity
(health care inflation has long outpaced general consumer price
inflation), by insufficient pension savings, and by concerns about
pension providers’ or governments’ ability to fulfill their (often
excessive) commitments. Given the large demographic changes
expected in the first half of the 21st century, these issues will surely
have a large impact on asset returns. Unfortunately, these important
topics are outside the scope of this book.

Dollar cost averaging. One common investment approach is to
invest a constant dollar amount into the equity market on a regular
basis. (This approach is called dollar cost averaging, because with a
constant outlay every month you end up buying more shares at low
prices and fewer at high prices.) The main advantage is that dollar cost
averaging gives discipline to an individual’s plan for saving by
investing in equities. It can also reduce the regret associated with
mistiming purchases and it may benefit from any mean reversion in
aggregate market valuations. However, excluding the mean reversion
argument (on which empirical evidence is weak), standard finance
theory says that dollar cost averaging is suboptimal. If investors
postpone equity investments despite having earned and saved a given
amount of cash income, they delay earning the equity premium and
thus reduce long-run returns. If the optimal portfolio is a given
combination of riskless assets and risky equity, theory advises instantly
allocating any new cash income to riskless and risky assets in these
optimal proportions. Newer ideas about time-varying risk premia may
justify the contrarian market timing implicit in dollar cost averaging,
but only for those investors who differ from the average market
participant. Finally, in a world of low risky asset premia, the benefits
of discipline come more cheaply.

28.4 SKILL

Finding skill

Besides trying to reap risk and illiquidity premia, trying to add value by
skillful active management is the other popular way to try to boost the
prospective returns on one’s portfolio. It is well known that beating the
market is difficult, but few deem it impossible. (Over 80% of U.S. equity
mutual fund assets are actively managed, and over 50% of institutional
mandates are for active management.) Traditionally, most academics argue
that 
markets 
are 
broadly 
efficient 
(see 
Section 
5.3), 
while 
many
practitioners laugh at the notion. In addition to deciding whether the market
is too efficient for them to bother to try to beat it, investors need to judge
their own ability to be among the winners in a negative-sum game.

Given that prior views on this question are so polarized, it is perhaps not
surprising that there are two public puzzles that ask virtually opposite
questions: (i) Why is most delegated portfolio management still allocated to
active managers? and (ii) Why is such a small share of institutional risk
budgets allocated to active managers?

• Equilibrium accounting shows that the sum of all active risk-
taking positions—deviations from a passive market portfolio—must
amount to zero. Thus, active management is a zero-sum game before
costs and fees, and a negative-sum game after they are included. Any
gains one active investor makes must come at the expense of a losing
active investor. The average active investor must deliver a lower net
return than the average passive investor who benefits from much lower
costs and fees. In jargon, the net alpha is negative.
• Robert Litterman’s “active risk puzzle” notes that only a small
share of institutional risk budgets is allocated to active management.
(Even when capital is allocated to long-only active managers, tracking
error targets tend to be much lower than the volatility of underlying
assets.) Given that equity market risk takes up 90% of many risk
budgets, and the related SR is about 0.3, the small share of active risk
must imply a much lower expected SR for active management. Yet,
virtually anyone marketing active management services promises a
higher SR, so a higher allocation to active risk should be easy to
justify.
The two puzzles address two distinct issues. The first puzzle is about the
inability of the average active investor to beat the market, while the second
one 
is 
about 
identifying 
superior 
managers 
(or 
being 
one 
oneself).
Overconfidence, 
salesmanship, 
and 
ignorance 
make 
most 
investors—
amateur or professional—believe that they can beat the market or select
winning managers. It is part of human nature that we believe that we are
above average like the mythical children of Lake Wobegon and, certainly,
all professional active managers (must) profess to be superior investors.
Only past winners market their track record; others toil silently or leave the
field. Most people prefer to overlook these facts.

Truly superior investors do exist, but they are rare, hard to find in
advance, and costly. Likewise, identifying winning individual securities
requires a lot of effort, or luck:
• There will always be some manager who is outperforming—and
a small minority have consistently succeeded over very long time
periods. Many academics argue that most superstars may just be lucky,
given 
the 
broad 
universe 
of 
candidates. 
Indeed, 
evidence 
on
performance 
persistence 
among 
mutual 
fund 
managers 
is 
scant,
although it is better among alternative asset managers.
• Manager return predictability is improved if there are shared
characteristics 
among 
the 
few 
consistent 
outperformers. 
Warren
Buffett famously responded to academic claims about lucky coin-
flippers that a disproportionately large number of winners in the
investment game are residents of Graham-and-Doddsville (i.e., value-
oriented investors). In contrast, successful market-timers are a rare
species. (Not even purists assign all the success of Warren Buffett to
luck rather than skill, but they may insist that individual superstars are
identified with hindsight. For example, the star status of Bill Miller
and Ken Griffin was questioned after both stumbled in 2008.)
• One promising shared characteristic is manager willingness to
take active risk instead of hugging the benchmark. There is evidence
that mutual funds with a high “active share” (fraction of portfolio that
differs from the benchmark index), or those with a low R
2
against the
market, tend to outperform peer funds. Also, in the HF space, the most
distinctive 
or 
crowd-bucking 
managers 
perform 
especially 
well.
Echoing these results, both mutual fund managers’ best ideas and HF
managers’ high-conviction trades demonstrably add value. So why do
managers with apparent stock-picking skills tend to underperform their
benchmarks? It is lukewarm positions added to the portfolio to boost
diversification and to reduce tracking error against the index that
appear to detract from value.
• Investor skill does not automatically ensure gains for end-
investors. The influential Berk–Green (2004) model of delegated asset
management implies that competition for skillful managers eliminates
any gains for investors in active funds even when skillful managers
and 
performance 
persistence 
exist. 
Investors 
correctly 
take 
past
outperformance as a signal of investment quality, but performance-
chasing demand allows fund managers to extract all the rents they can
from their skill; they raise their AUM until they no longer offer excess
profits after fees and transaction costs.

Life would be easier if some clear investor group could be identified as
exhibiting superior performance and steadily extracting alpha from other
market participants:
• The old idea was that professional investors outperform at the
expense of individual investors. However, hundreds of studies since
the 1960s confirm that the majority of professional active managers
underperform their benchmarks (not retail investors’ benchmarks, but
institutional investors’ own benchmarks). Most studies compare active
mutual fund manager returns with index returns; for example, Wermers
(2000) and Fama–French (2009) find that average returns in excess of
a relevant index are mildly positive before fees but negative after fees.
Taking a broader approach, French (2008) estimates various costs and
fees that the U.S. active equity management industry accrues; he
concludes that end-investors have on aggregate overspent about 70 bp
per year, compared with a passive alternative. The aggregate cost has
been surprisingly stable over time because lower trading costs have
been offset by a shift to higher fee products and higher turnover.
• Evidence is more mixed for managers of institutional funds.
These 
funds 
tend 
to 
outperform 
their 
mutual 
fund 
peers, 
likely
reflecting lower fees and lower agency costs. However, pension plan
sponsors do not exhibit manager selection skills; newly fired managers
tend 
to 
subsequently 
outperform 
newly 
hired 
managers 
[6].
Endowment funds show generally good performance and the largest
endowments have the best track records. This evidence is consistent
with the idea that large, resource-rich institutions can add value, but it
could also reflect the one-time effect of early entry into alternative
assets.
• Hedge funds are the prototypical skilled investors of the 21st
century. It looks like HFs can outperform even as a group by extracting
alpha 
from 
others—long-only 
institutions, 
retail 
investors,
corporations, and the public sector. Section 11.4.2 reviews various

investors with superior skills exist in this universe than anywhere else.

Alpha–beta separation and portable alpha have been popular themes during
the past decade. Traditional fund managers bundle alpha and beta together
at relatively unattractive combined costs. (Active long-only managers take
modest active risks and deliver even more modest active returns, but the
fees, while modest by alternative investment standards, are often shockingly
high as a proportion of value added, often actually exceeding the value
added.) End-investors found that by separating alpha bets and beta bets,
they 
could 
save 
costs 
and 
make 
more 
efficient 
risk

End-
investors have therefore consistently de-allocated capital from traditional
active managers and moved it toward an alpha–beta barbell (as well as to
alternative asset classes). HFs, ideally market-neutral ones, form the natural
alpha (skill) source, while low-fee index funds or index derivatives form the
natural source of beta (risk premia). The combined fees for this barbell can
be lower than for traditional managers, and combined after-fee returns
higher. There are limits to this trend, however: separating alpha and beta is
much harder, perhaps infeasible, in less liquid asset classes such as private
equity and real estate.

Applying skill

It is too simplistic to equate skills and HFs, alpha and HFs, or skills and
alpha. Not all HF managers are skilled and not all skilled investors work for
HFs. HFs do not only deliver alpha but also traditional and alternative betas
(see Section 11.4.3, which stresses that alpha is always defined in the
context of a factor model as the uncorrelated, unexplained part of returns).
Skill is not only used in alpha generation (narrowly defined as view taking)
but also in smart portfolio construction, risk management, and costefficient
implementation. Critics emphasize fees and agency problems which can
prevent the fruits of skillful management from reaching the end-investor.
Last but not least, ex post alpha reflects luck (randomness) as well as skill,
and possibly also overfitting or reporting biases. (For these reasons, it is
important 
to 
evaluate 
investments 
by 
the 
ex 
ante 
quality 
of 
decision
making, not just by ex post success.)

Skill can be applied in various ways. If we focus narrowly on view
taking, the classic distinction is between actively timing the market and
selecting 
securities, 
or, 
in 
between, 
allocating 
across 
asset 
classes,
countries, 
sectors, 
styles, 
or 
managers. 
Another 
distinction 
is 
between
discretionary and systematic managers, again with a fuzzy boundary. Most
managers 
use 
similar 
information—both 
fundamental 
and 
technical
indicators—but the discretionary ones make subjective decisions based on
them, while the systematic ones try to code up their week-to-week decision
rules. Finally, skill can be applied in in-house active management or in
selecting external managers.

Skill does not alone determine expected value-added; one also needs
opportunities to apply any skills. Mechanically, the fundamental law of
active management says that expected risk-adjusted return depends on
investing skill and the breadth of the opportunity set to which the skill is
applied. For a single trade, the expected risk-adjusted alpha depends on skill
and signal strength (size of the current opportunity). Discretionary traders
quickly 
dilute 
their 
skill 
when 
they 
widen 
their 
interests. 
Systematic
investors 
can 
better 
exploit 
breadth 
(with 
their 
admittedly 
narrower
information set).

Where is skill best applied? A common view is that alpha is best found in
illiquid markets with inefficiently priced assets—where assets are harder to
value, information travels more slowly, mispricings survive longer, and
arbitrage is more difficult (because of high trading costs and a lack of
substitutes). An alternative view favors liquid markets, because in illiquid
markets it takes a lifetime to distinguish a skillful alpha producer from a
lucky one, or from an investor whose apparent alpha is really beta. In
contrast, frequent performance measurement in liquid assets enables skill to
be differentiated from luck over a reasonable evaluation period. Moreover,
only liquid markets give investors the flexibility to terminate, at a moderate
cost, a manager that has disappointed.

28.5 COSTS

Cost control is important because fees and costs feed directly to the bottom
line of net returns. John Bogle likes to stress that cost differences across
mutual funds can explain differences in net fund returns much better than
any other feature can. (Among HFs, fee differences do not explain net
return differences.) I review in Section 28.1 various ways to reduce costs
but I also note that the virtue can be taken too far. The costs of systematic
investment mistakes can be much higher than direct trading costs and fees;
recall the examples of “dumb money” in the previous subsection (see Note
6).

For small investors, explicit costs, such as commissions and bid–ask
spreads, 
tend 
to 
dominate. 
For 
large 
investors, 
implicit 
costs 
(market
impact, front-running, etc.) matter more.

External manager contracts involve relatively straightforward fixed fees
and performance fees but also less visible costs related to asymmetric
incentives, high-water marks, lockups, and other compensation specifics
with HFs, and an even greater variety of costs with PE funds.

Trading costs are naturally higher for less liquid assets and for strategies
that involve greater turnover. Trading only the most liquid assets and using
buy-and-hold strategies are two ways to keep costs down—but they may
keep returns down as well.

Trading costs influence the relative attractiveness of active styles by
causing the tradeoff between horizon and signal decay to differ between
styles. Persistent (slow-moving) value and carry signals always require less
turnover 
than 
fast-decaying 
momentum/reversal 
and
underreaction/overreaction 
signals. 
The 
former 
can 
be 
traded 
patiently
without much signal decay (long-horizon trading); the latter require speed
(short-horizon trading). All else equal, rising trading costs make patient
trading more optimal and should result in persistent signals having a larger
weight. In reality, falling trading costs have made high-frequency trading
more economical, but only for market participants facing the very lowest
trading costs, since before-fee profits from high-frequency trading tend to
be razor-thin.

Long-horizon investors often ignore information from high-frequency
signals—such as reversal, momentum, or seasonal effects—due to the
turnover required. New research by Israelov–Katz (2010) shows that even
long-horizon 
investors 
can 
benefit 
from 
such 
signals 
by 
delaying 
or
avoiding trading implementation when “fast” signals counsel patience.

Urgency of implementation and demand for liquidity are linked at the
hip. Liquid assets satisfy urgency needs, illiquid ones do not. One way to
reveal urgency and demand liquidity is to place a market order. Conversely,
limit orders can be used to supply liquidity (although care is needed with
limit orders; recent research shows that stale limit orders are among the key
reasons 
for 
retail 
investors’ 
“dumb” 
losses). 
Demanding 
liquidity 
is
especially 
costly 
when 
market 
volatility 
is 
high; 
conversely, 
liquidity
provision 
is 
better 
rewarded 
amidst 
turbulent 
conditions. 
Short-term
reversal 
strategies 
are 
one 
way 
to 
supply 
liquidity 
to 
other 
market
participants; such strategies exploit temporary price dislocations due to
large traders’ market impact. The performance of such reversal strategies
comoves closely with the VIX equity volatility index, which is a decent
proxy for the market’s liquidity conditions.

Patient trading may also reduce market impact costs as long as other
players (counterparties, high-frequency traders, and competitors) are not
able to predict and front-run your flows. And it is not only your own trading
that matters. You can save on market impact costs by avoiding trading that
is synchronous with that of other large players: for example, you should not
rebalance at month ends, or trade popular index products, when the crowd
is likely to act. As already noted, even so-called passive management can
suffer from hidden costs. Exact index matching can be detrimental due to
price pressure effects when many index holders synchronously buy (sell)
index 
additions 
(deletions). 
You 
can 
improve 
long-run 
returns 
if 
you
customize benchmarks and execution strategies that do not mechanically
trade together with the index crowd.
And a final mishmash of advice:
• Do not naively think that high fees are justified. Invest in costly
products with care and thought (e.g., pay high fees only for credibly
expected 
alpha). 
Pay 
less 
for 
alternative 
beta 
and 
even 
less 
for
traditional bulk beta. Pay lower fees if you have to agree to lockups
and other liquidity-hampering restrictions that favor the manager.
• Think twice before paying up for bad economic reasons, such as
avoiding mark to market or earning high upfront yield. Do not ignore
taxes (as this book by and large does; see Box 28.4).
• New alternative trading platforms and electronic trading venues
are worth exploring.

Box 28.4. Taxes

Taxes are a form of costs or slippage. This book ignores taxes, even
though taxable investors should care only or mostly about (real) after-
tax expected returns. Tax effects can even influence assets’ pre-tax
expected returns. Here are some examples of tax effects on financial
markets:
• Taxation causes many forms of market segmentation.
Certain assets—municipal bonds, low-coupon bonds, and zero-
dividend stocks—are especially attractive for investors with high
tax rates, which makes these assets overvalued from the
perspective of non-taxable investors.
• The income tax rate is often higher than the capital gains
tax rate. Even at the same tax rate, capital gains are a more
attractive source of revenue than income because they allow
greater tax flexibility. Investors can defer realizing gains and
paying capital gains taxes, while harvesting capital losses that
may offset other taxable income. Active strategies that involve
high turnover are less tax efficient than passive strategies that
delay taxation.
• Changes in tax laws over time can influence absolute and
relative pricing. Falling tax rates from the 1980s to the 2000s
contributed to rising equity market valuations, while the dividend
tax cut in 2003 boosted demand for high-dividend-yield stocks.
• Mortgage interest rate deductibility influences both bond
and real estate pricing. The yield spreads of mortgages and
corporates vs. Treasuries partly reflect differential (state and
local) tax treatment. Tax-loss selling contributes to turn-of-the-
year and January effects.

[1] Clearly, the riskiness of individual investments is not well captured by
standalone volatility, which ignores their diversification abilities and timing
of losses. For a portfolio, however, volatility could be a sufficient risk
statistic. In standard mean variance analysis, a portfolio is optimal if it has
the highest Sharpe ratio of those from which it is possible to choose.

[2] The carry style proxy is a composite of yield-seeking strategies in
currency and fixed income markets. The trend style proxy is a composite of
trend-following strategies in dozens of liquid assets from four asset classes:
bond 
or 
interest 
rate 
futures, 
currencies, 
equity 
country 
futures, 
and
commodity futures. These style proxies are presented in Chapters 13 and
14.

[3] Estimated trading costs, but not fees, have been subtracted from returns
on style proxies. Net-of-fee returns of the main CTA indices imply long-run
Sharpe ratios of 0.2 to 0.5, clearly lower than our trend style proxy.

[4] Defenders of diversification rightly stress that even if in systemic crises
diversification temporarily fails “just when it is most needed”, it still works
well in the long run and really serves to reduce a portfolio’s long-run
volatility, which should matter more for long-horizon investors.

[5] However, state-of-the-art literature on long-horizon investing—such as
Campbell and Viceira’s (2002) work—does not capture the intuition that
long-horizon investors can, like Warren Buffett, ride through short-term
market fluctuations better than myopic investors, and thus also serve as
opportunistic value buyers. Instead, intertemporal hedging demands can
cause long-horizon investors in these models to be even more sensitive to
changing opportunities than myopic investors are. Perhaps the reason is that
this literature relies on utility functions without time-varying risk aversion.
In reality, the key distinction seems to be that most investors exhibit wealth-
dependent risk aversion (which would discourage them from buying more
risky assets in bad times when time-varying risk premia are elevated)
whereas long-horizon investors may have more stable risk preferences over
time (and would derive utility gains from buying low-priced risky assets in
bad times).

[6] A parallel finding is that investors in mutual funds and HFs hurt their
long-run performance by poor timing and/or allocation decisions, likely by
performance chasing at horizons where the reversal effect has overtaken
momentum (buying multi-year winners and selling multi-year losers). As a
result, dollar-weighted returns (which reflect inflows and outflows and
better capture actual returns that fund investors have earned) are much
lower than the same funds’ time-weighted or buy-and-hold returns (which
reflect average manager performance). Some estimate the gap at 1.5% for
investors in mutual funds and at 3% to 7% (see Section 11.4.2) for investors
in HFs. Such evidence (plus the stale limit order effect, overtrading, etc.)
are used as justifications for arguing that smart investors have a great deal
of opportunity to extract alpha from “dumb money” investors even given
the zero-sum nature of overall active management.
